1. Q: What is artificial intelligence according to Margaret A. Boden’s opening chapter?
A: Show answer
Boden describes artificial intelligence as the attempt to make computers perform the kinds of things minds can do. The relevant activities include familiar forms of reasoning but also perception, association, prediction, planning, and motor control. Some of these abilities are not usually labelled intelligent in everyday conversation, yet they are psychological capacities that help humans and animals attain goals. The definition is deliberately broad: it directs attention away from a single test of intelligence and toward the diverse information-processing abilities that support intelligent behavior.

3. Q: Why does the chapter describe intelligence as a richly structured space of information-processing capacities?
A: Show answer
The chapter rejects the idea that intelligence is one simple dimension. Cognitive performance draws on multiple capacities that may be combined in different ways depending on the task, including perception, learning, memory, prediction, planning, and movement. An AI system may be strong in one capacity and weak in another, so a single label can hide important differences. This view explains why AI uses many techniques rather than one universal algorithm. It also foreshadows the book’s discussion of generality: a broadly capable system must coordinate different processes, not merely optimize one narrow skill.

6. Q: What are the two main aims of artificial intelligence identified by Boden?
A: Show answer
Boden distinguishes a technological aim from a scientific aim. The technological aim is to use computers to get useful things done, sometimes through methods very unlike those used by biological minds. The scientific aim is to use AI concepts and computational models to answer questions about humans and other living organisms. Some researchers focus mainly on building useful systems, while others use computational ideas to understand cognition, behavior, or life. The distinction matters because a system can be technologically successful without being a faithful model of the human mind, and a scientific model can be informative without being the most efficient practical product.

12. Q: What does Boden mean by a virtual machine in the context of AI?
A: Show answer
A virtual machine is the information-processing system that a programmer designs or a user understands the software to be performing. It is not a machine shown in virtual reality or necessarily a separate physical device. It is an organized level of computational activity, such as a word processor’s operations on documents or a neural network’s distributed pattern processing. Boden emphasizes that the physical computer is not the main explanatory level for every question. To understand an AI program, researchers often need to analyze the operations, representations, and causal interactions of the software system implemented on the hardware.

28. Q: What are cellular automata, and why are they relevant to AI and artificial life?
A: Show answer
Cellular automata are systems composed of many computational units whose states change according to simple rules involving their current state and the states of neighboring units. Although individual rules may be simple, repeated local interactions can produce complex global patterns. Boden discusses their use in modeling development, self-organization, and biological evolution, including von Neumann’s investigations of replication. Their relevance is that complex behavior need not always be programmed as a detailed sequence of central instructions; it can emerge from many local interactions. The model’s assumptions and rule design remain crucial.
