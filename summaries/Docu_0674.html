2. Q: What does Kandel mean when he describes memory as mental time travel?

Mental time travel is the capacity to mentally revisit an earlier experience while remaining in the present. A person can recall not only that an event happened but also its setting, sensory details, social relationships, and emotional atmosphere. Kandel illustrates this with memories of his childhood in Vienna, which he can revisit decades later while writing in New York. The phrase is metaphorical, not a claim that consciousness literally travels through time. It emphasizes how memory connects present thought with an internally reconstructed representation of the past. This capacity supports personal continuity, planning, learning from experience, and the ability to place current events in a wider life story.

17. Q: What did brain imaging add to cognitive neuroscience?
A: Show answer
The chapter describes brain imaging as allowing researchers to observe changes associated with brain activity while people perform tasks. Positron-emission tomography, or PET, measures aspects of energy use, including glucose metabolism in particular protocols. Functional magnetic resonance imaging, or fMRI, commonly measures blood-oxygenation changes associated indirectly with neural activity. These methods made it possible to investigate brain activity during perception, spatial thinking, memory, and voluntary action. They do not photograph thoughts or directly reveal the meaning of a person's experience. Imaging signals are indirect, depend on experimental design and analysis, and must be interpreted alongside behavior and other evidence.

18. Q: What are the main limitations of interpreting PET and fMRI images?
A: Show answer
An image showing activity in a region does not prove that the region alone performs a mental function or that the activity is the cause of a behavior. PET and fMRI measure physiological signals related to neural activity, not subjective experience itself. Results depend on task design, comparison conditions, spatial and temporal resolution, statistical choices, and the population studied. A region may participate in several processes, and a mental task usually recruits networks rather than one isolated site. Researchers therefore compare conditions, consider alternative explanations, replicate findings, and combine imaging with behavioral, clinical, electrophysiological, or other evidence. A critical reading should avoid treating colorful maps as self-explanatory.
