Consumable and Equipment Classification
GOVERNING PRINCIPLE

• Never classify an item solely from its name. First determine what the item does, how long it is expected to remain usable, whether it is normally consumed or repeatedly used, whether it retains its identity, whether it is controlled individually, whether it meets the organisation's capitalization threshold, and what the applicable donor agreement requires.
• “Consumable” and “equipment” are operational classifications; “expense,” “inventory,” “capital asset,” “fixed asset,” “supplies,” and “intangible asset” are accounting/reporting classifications. They are related but are not interchangeable.
• A durable item can be equipment even when its cost is below the NGO's capitalization threshold. In that situation it may be treated as a low-value/non-capital equipment item for accounting while remaining equipment for procurement, custody, and donor reporting.
• An item can be physically durable yet still be treated as an expense rather than a fixed asset because the organisation's capitalization policy sets a monetary threshold, provided the treatment is consistent with the applicable accounting framework and donor rules.
• A high-value item is not automatically equipment merely because it is expensive. A service, subscription, consultancy, consumable stock, construction activity, lease, intangible asset, or prepaid expense may be high-value without being equipment.
• A low-value item is not automatically a consumable. A $20 calculator may be durable equipment operationally even if the NGO expenses it under its low-value asset policy.
• “Consumed” means more than “used.” An item is generally consumable when its substance, functionality, quantity, or identity is substantially exhausted through normal operations or when it is normally issued for one-time/short-term use.
• “Equipment” normally means a tangible, durable, non-expendable item that remains identifiable and usable after repeated operations and is expected to serve beyond a single routine use. Government procurement rules similarly define equipment around durability, non-expendability, functional completeness, and retention of identity.
• Under IAS 16, property, plant and equipment are tangible items held for use and expected to be used during more than one period. This is an accounting concept, not a universal donor procurement label.
• IAS 16 also says that spare parts, standby equipment, and servicing equipment are recognised as PPE when they meet the PPE definition; otherwise they are classified as inventory. Therefore “spare part” does not automatically mean consumable.
• The first classification question is physical durability: “Will the item normally continue to perform its intended function after ordinary use?” If no, classify as consumable/supply unless a specific donor or sector rule says otherwise.
• The second question is expected useful life: “Is it normally expected to remain usable beyond one operating period or repeated service cycle?” A yes answer strongly supports equipment/non-consumable treatment, subject to the accounting threshold and donor rules.
• The third question is identity: “Does the item remain a recognizable standalone item after use?” If it disappears into the process, is incorporated into another item, or is physically consumed, consumable/material treatment is normally appropriate.
• The fourth question is repeatability: “Can the same physical item be used repeatedly for its intended purpose?” Repeated reusable use strongly supports equipment or another durable non-consumable category.
• The fifth question is custody: “Would management reasonably expect the item to be assigned, tagged, tracked, safeguarded, or returned?” A yes answer strongly supports non-consumable/equipment control.
• The sixth question is replacement frequency. Items normally replaced after one use, one patient, one activity, one field distribution, or a short operational cycle generally behave as consumables.
• The seventh question is materiality. If an item is durable but below the capitalization threshold, do not automatically relabel it as a consumable; distinguish “non-capital equipment” from “consumable.”
• The eighth question is donor treatment. A donor may require equipment to be separately budgeted, prior-approved, inventoried, tagged, or reported even when the NGO's general ledger expenses the item immediately.
• The ninth question is ownership/title. Donor-funded equipment may remain NGO property, donor property, government property, beneficiary property, or another party's property depending on the agreement. Ownership does not by itself determine whether something is equipment.
• The tenth question is grant attribution. An item can be equipment in nature but funded from several grants, shared across programmes, purchased centrally, or transferred between projects. Classification and funding allocation must therefore be recorded separately.
• The eleventh question is intended recipient. Equipment retained by the NGO is different from durable goods purchased for distribution to beneficiaries. A bicycle distributed to a beneficiary is still a durable good operationally, but may not become an NGO fixed asset.
• The twelfth question is purpose. The same physical item can have different accounting/budget treatment depending on whether it is for internal operations, programme implementation, resale/distribution, construction, rental, demonstration, or beneficiary transfer.
• The thirteenth question is whether the item forms part of a larger system. A component can be a consumable/material when used in production or repair, but a major separately identifiable component may require equipment/PPE treatment.
• The fourteenth question is whether the item is normally stocked. Frequently stocked items can still be equipment; stock status is not sufficient to make an item a consumable.
• The fifteenth question is whether the item is merely a replacement part. Ordinary replacement parts are generally materials/spares; major replacement components with significant useful lives may be capitalised depending on the accounting policy.

• Recommended rule for “best master classification” — Do not create only two categories called CONSUMABLE and EQUIPMENT. For a mature multi-donor NGO, use at least CONSUMABLE, DURABLE SUPPLY, EQUIPMENT, INVENTORY-DISTRIBUTION, MATERIAL, SERVICE, SOFTWARE/INTANGIBLE, VEHICLE, INFRASTRUCTURE/PROPERTY, and OTHER ASSET.
• Recommended rule for “simple two-way report” — When a donor or management report requires only two categories, map the detailed internal taxonomy into CONSUMABLE versus EQUIPMENT after classification, rather than using the two-way report as the underlying accounting master.
• Recommended two-way mapping — CONSUMABLE should normally include items consumed, depleted, expended, discarded, incorporated, or routinely replaced through ordinary use, while EQUIPMENT should normally include durable, reusable, separately identifiable physical items retained for repeated use.
• Recommended two-way mapping exception — Durable beneficiary-distribution goods should normally be mapped separately as PROGRAMME DISTRIBUTION internally, even if a simplified donor report subsequently maps them to either supplies or equipment.
• Recommended two-way mapping exception — Buildings, infrastructure, land, software, leases, services, vehicles, and beneficiary transfers should not be forced into consumable/equipment internally merely because a report has only two columns.
• Recommended two-way mapping for low-value equipment — Map to EQUIPMENT operationally, while accounting treatment may be EXPENSE. This preserves correct asset-control logic.
• Recommended two-way mapping for inventory — Map disposable inventory to CONSUMABLE and durable goods held for beneficiary distribution to PROGRAMME DISTRIBUTION rather than pretending that all inventory is consumable.
• Recommended two-way mapping for repair components — Routine wear parts can map to CONSUMABLE/MAINTENANCE MATERIAL; major components requiring capital treatment should map to EQUIPMENT/PPE or COMPONENT.
• Recommended two-way mapping for fuel — CONSUMABLE.
• Recommended two-way mapping for food and medicine — CONSUMABLE/INVENTORY.
• Recommended two-way mapping for paper/toner/ink — CONSUMABLE.
• Recommended two-way mapping for laptops/printers/phones/generators/cameras — EQUIPMENT.
• Recommended two-way mapping for furniture — EQUIPMENT/DURABLE SUPPLY.
• Recommended two-way mapping for reusable PPE — EQUIPMENT/DURABLE SUPPLY.
• Recommended two-way mapping for disposable PPE — CONSUMABLE.
• Recommended two-way mapping for major spare parts — CASE-BY-CASE based on useful life and accounting policy.
• Recommended two-way mapping for software — NOT EQUIPMENT in the underlying taxonomy; map separately as software/intangible/service.
• Recommended two-way mapping for cloud subscriptions — NOT EQUIPMENT; service/software.
• Recommended two-way mapping for consultancy — NOT EQUIPMENT; service.
• Recommended two-way mapping for construction materials — MATERIAL/PROJECT COST, not equipment.
• Recommended two-way mapping for buildings — PROPERTY/INFRASTRUCTURE, not equipment.
• Recommended two-way mapping for vehicles — EQUIPMENT/VEHICLE under many operational taxonomies, but preserve VEHICLE as a separate class.
• Recommended two-way mapping for leases — ROU ASSET/LEASE or RENTAL, not ordinary equipment.
• Recommended two-way mapping for beneficiary transfers — PROGRAMME DISTRIBUTION, not NGO equipment unless control remains with NGO.
• Final decision test — Ask in this order: “Is it a service/intangible/property rather than a tangible operating item?”; “Is it consumed, depleted, incorporated or discarded through normal use?”; “Is it designed for repeated use?”; “Does it retain its identity?”; “Will NGO control/custody continue?”; “Is it held for internal use or distribution?”; “Does it meet the NGO capitalization policy?”; “Does the donor impose a different budget/control definition?”
FINAL RULE

• If the answer is “used up/issued/discarded/incorporated” → normally CONSUMABLE/MATERIAL/INVENTORY.
• If the answer is “reusable, durable, identifiable, retained and repeatedly used” → normally EQUIPMENT/DURABLE SUPPLY.
• If the answer is “durable but below capitalization threshold” → EQUIPMENT/DURABLE SUPPLY operationally; EXPENSE accounting treatment may apply.
• If the answer is “durable but permanently transferred to a beneficiary/partner” → PROGRAMME DISTRIBUTION/DURABLE TRANSFER, not necessarily NGO equipment.
• If the answer is “used to create another asset and loses its identity” → MATERIAL/PROJECT COST.
• If the answer is “software, subscription, consultancy, rent, insurance, transport, training or another service” → SERVICE/INTANGIBLE/LEASE category, not consumable/equipment.
• If the answer is “high-value stock that is consumed or distributed” → INVENTORY/CONSUMABLE; high value does not make it equipment.
• If the answer is “major spare/standby/service item with multi-period useful life” → evaluate for PPE/equipment rather than automatically treating it as inventory. IAS 16 explicitly requires this fact-specific assessment.
• If the answer changes because of the donor, do not change the underlying physical classification unnecessarily. Maintain an internal nature classification and a separate donor mapping.
• For a multi-donor NGO, the cleanest architecture is therefore: ITEM NATURE → ACCOUNTING TREATMENT → DONOR BUDGET CATEGORY → DONOR CONTROL → OWNERSHIP/TITLE → FUNDING SOURCE → CUSTODIAN/LOCATION → CAPITALISATION STATUS → DISPOSITION.
• The single most important distinction is: “expensed” does not mean “consumable,” and “capitalised” does not mean “equipment.” These are different dimensions and should not be collapsed into one field.
