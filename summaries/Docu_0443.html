Accounting: Foundations, Professional Practice, and Reporting Entities
1. Q: What is the purpose of accounting and what is the purpose of this book's introduction to the subject?
A: Show answer
Accounting creates organized records and information about an entity's transactions, resources, obligations, performance, and financial position. That information supports decisions by owners, managers, investors, lenders, public authorities, employees, and other users. Chapter 1 is written for the intelligent non-specialist: it explains the language and uses of accounting rather than promising to turn the reader into a professional accountant. It also provides a foundation for later academic or professional study. The distinction matters because useful accounting literacy is broader than the accounting profession; people can make better decisions when they understand what financial information can and cannot tell them.
Study instruction:
- Explain the difference between learning to interpret accounting information and qualifying to perform professional accounting work. Identify three decisions in which basic accounting literacy would help.
Key point:
- Accounting organizes financial information for decisions and accountability.
- The chapter develops informed understanding, not professional qualification.
2. Q: Why does the chapter describe its intended reader as an intelligent non-specialist?
A: Show answer
Many people encounter accounting information without being accountants. Shareholders receive annual reports, managers review organizational information, committee members examine accounts, and individuals hear terms such as liability, asset, earnings, debits, pre-tax income, and goodwill in news or business discussions. These readers need enough knowledge to understand the meaning of the information, ask informed questions, and recognize when a decision depends on financial evidence. The chapter therefore assumes general intelligence and curiosity, not prior technical training. Its approach also recognizes that accounting terminology can create an unnecessary barrier when readers are unfamiliar with the concepts behind the words.
Study instruction:
- List the accounting information encountered by an investor, manager, employee, committee member, and consumer. State what each person needs to understand before acting on it.
Key point:
- Accounting information is relevant well beyond the accounting department.
- Clear explanations make financial information more accessible to non-specialists.
3. Q: Why should non-specialists learn the meaning of basic accounting terms?
A: Show answer
Terms such as asset, liability, earnings, debit, credit, goodwill, and pre-tax income summarize ideas that influence judgments about an organization. If a reader treats a liability as simply a negative description of a person, or assumes that earnings and cash are interchangeable, the reader may misunderstand a financial report or make an unsound decision. Understanding terminology does not require mastering every technical rule. It requires knowing what the term broadly represents, where it appears in financial information, and what questions should be asked before drawing a conclusion. The chapter's use of a glossary reinforces the value of checking unfamiliar terms rather than guessing from everyday meanings.
Study instruction:
- Build a personal glossary of unfamiliar accounting terms and write one practical example for each term using its accounting meaning rather than its everyday meaning.
Key point:
- Technical terms carry specific meanings that may differ from ordinary usage.
- Clarifying terminology is a first step toward interpreting financial information accurately.
4. Q: What does the chapter suggest about the historical relationship between accounting, writing, and numerals?
A: Show answer
The chapter observes that many early written records found by archaeologists are accounting records. It refers to research suggesting that the need to keep accounts was a major driver in the development of writing and numbers. The point is not that every writing system arose for accounting alone, but that societies needed durable ways to record quantities, exchanges, property, and obligations. Record-keeping helped people administer resources beyond what could be remembered or communicated informally. Accounting is therefore presented as an old social practice connected with the development of organized economic activity, rather than as a modern invention created only for corporations.
Study instruction:
- Explain how recording quantities and obligations could create demand for symbols, written records, and shared numerical conventions.
Key point:
- Early accounting records illustrate the long history of systematic resource tracking.
- Record-keeping helped support increasingly complex social and economic activity.

The argument is deliberately broad: accounting is not important only because businesses need profit information. 8. Q: What broad types of accounting activity does Chapter 1 distinguish?
A: Show answer
The chapter distinguishes bookkeeping, financial accounting, auditing, and management accounting. Bookkeeping records transactions and supplies raw data. Financial accounting uses accounting data to report an entity's cash flows, profit, and financial position, commonly in summarized reports for external users. Auditing involves independent checking of information reported by management. Management accounting uses more detailed information internally to support decisions and control. These activities are connected, but they are not interchangeable: recording a transaction, preparing external reports, independently examining those reports, and using detailed internal information are different tasks with different users and purposes.

9. Q: What is bookkeeping and why does it precede other accounting activities?
A: Show answer
Bookkeeping is the process of recording transactions and other relevant events so that the entity has an organized record of what has occurred. A shop sale, a bank loan, and a manufacturer's wage payment are examples of events that need to be recorded. The resulting records provide the raw data used to prepare financial information, analyze costs, monitor budgets, and support other accounting work. If transactions are omitted, duplicated, recorded in the wrong period, or described inaccurately, later reports and analyses may also be unreliable. Bookkeeping is therefore a foundation for accounting, even though it is not the whole of accounting.
Study instruction:
- Trace a simple sale from the underlying transaction evidence to its bookkeeping record and identify the information needed for later reporting.
Key point:
- Bookkeeping captures transaction data for subsequent accounting use.
- Incomplete or inaccurate records weaken every process that depends on them.

The record should make it possible to identify what happened, when it happened, the parties or accounts involved, and the amount or value. The examples show that bookkeeping is not limited to cash sales or to one industry. Any organization that exchanges goods, receives financing, pays staff, or otherwise conducts transactions needs records that can be assembled and analyzed consistently.

11. Q: What is double-entry bookkeeping and what historical point does the chapter make about it?
A: Show answer
Double-entry bookkeeping is the recording technique identified by the chapter as the method used in nearly all bookkeeping, whether records are kept on paper or in a computer system. It is associated with debits and credits and is described as an Italian invention dating from the thirteenth century. At an introductory level, the important lesson is that bookkeeping is not simply a list of cash receipts and payments: transactions are recorded through a structured system of entries that preserves relationships among accounts. The chapter introduces the technique rather than teaching the detailed mechanics of debit and credit rules in this section.

Key point:
- Double-entry bookkeeping is the principal recording technique identified in the chapter.

12. Q: How has computerization changed bookkeeping and what remains essential?
A: Show answer
The chapter explains that bookkeeping was historically performed using journals and ledgers on paper but is now generally carried out by computer. Computerization changes the medium and can make recording, processing, retrieval, and summarization more efficient. It does not remove the underlying need to record transactions accurately or eliminate the logic of double-entry bookkeeping. A computer system can process incorrect, incomplete, or misleading input just as a paper process can. The reliability of the resulting information still depends on the quality of source documents, classification, data entry, system configuration, and review controls.
Study instruction:
- Compare a paper journal-and-ledger process with a computerized bookkeeping system. Identify which tasks change and which accuracy and review requirements remain.
Key point:
- Computerization changes the recording method, not the need for sound accounting records.
- Automated processing cannot by itself guarantee accurate input or classification.
13. Q: What is financial accounting according to Chapter 1?
A: Show answer
Financial accounting is the use of accounting data to calculate and report an entity's cash flows, profit, and financial position. It transforms individual records into information that can be understood by users who need an overall view of the entity. The chapter uses the broad term entity so that the definition is not restricted to private companies. Financial accounting is therefore concerned with the preparation of summarized financial information, rather than only recording individual transactions or preparing internal product-level analyses. Its outputs help users evaluate financial performance and position and make decisions about the entity.

15. Q: What is financial reporting and how does it relate to financial accounting?
A: Show answer
Financial accounting is the use of accounting data to calculate and report financial information; financial reporting is the process of communicating that information to users. The chapter emphasizes that the information is usually summarized for people outside the entity, such as shareholders. The reporting process makes the entity's financial results and position available to people who are not involved in day-to-day bookkeeping or management. Financial reporting is therefore not merely the production of numbers: it is the communication of financial information in a form intended to support evaluation, accountability, and decisions.

16. Q: Who are external users of financial reports and why do they need summarized information?
A: Show answer
External users include shareholders and other people who need to assess an entity but do not manage its daily operations. The chapter uses shareholders as an example: they need information to evaluate the success of the company and its managers and to make investment decisions. A complete list of every transaction would be difficult to use for those purposes, so financial reporting presents accounting information in summarized form. Summary does not mean that detail is unimportant; underlying records must still support the reported totals. The challenge is to provide information that is sufficiently concise for users while remaining reliable and meaningful.

17. Q: What financial information does the chapter identify as central to financial accounting?
A: Show answer
The chapter's definition identifies cash flows, profit, and financial position. These are related but distinct views of an entity. Cash-flow information concerns movements of cash; profit concerns the results measured for a period under the applicable accounting basis; and financial position concerns the entity's resources and obligations at a point in time. The chapter mentions the income statement and balance sheet as financial reports. Readers should not assume that one measure answers every question: an entity may report profit while experiencing cash pressure, or hold valuable assets while also having substantial liabilities.

19. Q: What is auditing and why does the chapter say independent experts are needed?

Auditing is the independent examination of information reported by an entity's managers. In many organizations, owners are not the people who manage daily operations. Owners and other external users therefore rely on reports prepared by people whose decisions and performance they cannot observe directly. The chapter highlights the risk that managers, including accountants employed by the company, may overstate success or conceal problems. Independent experts are needed to check the reported information and provide users with greater confidence in it. The chapter introduces the purpose of auditing; detailed audit procedures and professional standards require further study.

20. Q: What reporting risk arises when managers prepare information for shareholders and other outsiders?

Managers have access to information about the entity and are responsible for preparing reports, while external users may have limited ability to verify the information themselves. This creates a risk that reported success will be overstated or problems will be hidden. The chapter uses this risk to explain why independent checking is important. It does not imply that every manager acts dishonestly; rather, it recognizes that users need a credible process for examining information prepared by people who have responsibility for the entity's performance. Strong records, review, governance, and independent audit can help address the information imbalance.

21. Q: How does auditing relate to the separation between ownership and management?

In many entities, owners provide capital or hold ownership rights but do not make every daily operating decision. Managers control operations and prepare information about results. Because owners are not continuously present to observe transactions and decisions, they need reports that fairly communicate what has happened. Auditing provides an independent check on the information prepared by management and helps owners evaluate stewardship. The need for audit is therefore connected to the organizational separation of ownership and control, not simply to the size of the bookkeeping function. Management accounting uses accounting data to provide detailed information for people inside an organization. Unlike external financial reporting, management accounting can be tailored to the needs and circumstances of the particular organization. 23. Q: Why can management accounting be tailored to an organization's particular circumstances?
A: Show answer
Management accounting is prepared for internal use, so the organization can design reports around its own products, factories, managers, processes, and decisions. Managers can choose the level of detail, frequency, and format that best supports operational decisions, provided the information is useful and reliable. The chapter contrasts this flexibility with external financial reporting, which serves a broader user group and therefore benefits from common rules. Tailoring does not mean that internal information can be careless; it must still be accurate enough for the decisions it supports.

Budgetary control identifies inputs and outputs with particular managers to help them make operational decisions. 26. Q: What is the difference between financial accounting and management accounting?
A: Show answer
Financial accounting prepares summarized information about an entity's cash flows, profit, and financial position for external users such as shareholders. Management accounting provides more detailed information for internal managers who make decisions and control operations. Financial accounting is influenced by external reporting requirements because many users need information that is comparable and credible. Management accounting can be designed to suit the entity's own needs. The two activities often use the same underlying bookkeeping data, but they organize and present it differently because their audiences and purposes differ. One should not be treated as a substitute for the other.

32. Q: Why does the chapter warn that international comparisons of accountancy bodies are difficult?
A: Show answer
Membership categories and professional roles are not necessarily comparable across countries. The chapter gives the example of a German body whose members are practising auditors, compared with the United Kingdom's Chartered Institute of Management Accountants, which has very few auditors and only where they also belong to another body. A comparison based only on membership totals could therefore give a misleading impression of the relative size or importance of the profession. Before comparing professional bodies, readers need to understand what qualifies a person for membership, which activities members perform, and whether the body covers the same professional population in each country.
Study instruction:

Key point:
- Membership statistics are meaningful only when the populations being counted are comparable.
- Professional titles and roles differ across countries.

Current classification is not a guarantee of immediate liquidity: inventory may be slow-moving and receivables may be doubtful.

10. Q: Why do accounting standards use different measurement bases for different assets?
A: Show answer
No single measurement basis answers every economic question equally well. Historical cost records an amount based on the transaction that created the asset. Fair value estimates a market-based exit price at the measurement date. Net realizable value estimates the proceeds from ordinary-course sale after completion and selling costs. Value in use estimates the present value of future cash flows expected from continuing use and eventual disposal. A machine used in production may be more informative at depreciated cost, while a quoted share may have an observable market price. 11. Q: How do net realizable value, fair value, historical cost, and value in use differ?
A: Show answer

Fair value is a market-participant exit price in an orderly transaction at the measurement date. NRV is particularly relevant to inventory; fair value is used when another Standard requires or permits it; historical cost is common for operating assets; and value in use is one component of impairment testing. 12. Q: Why is historical cost widely used, and what are its main limitations for investors?
A: Show answer
Historical cost is often verifiable, relatively inexpensive to establish, and less dependent on subjective market or cash-flow forecasts than some current-value measures. It can therefore provide a disciplined transaction-based starting point. Its limitation is that an old cost may become less relevant as prices, technology, market conditions, and the asset’s remaining service potential change. Two otherwise similar companies that acquired assets at different dates may report different carrying amounts. Historical cost can also obscure economic gains or losses that have not been recognized under the applicable Standard. Investors should examine depreciation, impairment, fair-value disclosures, asset age, and replacement needs rather than assuming that book value equals current economic value.

13. Q: How is investment property treated differently from owner-occupied property under IFRS?

14. Q: What costs are included in the cost of inventory under IAS 2?
A: Show answer
Inventory cost includes costs of purchase, costs of conversion, and other costs incurred in bringing inventory to its present location and condition. Purchase costs include the purchase price, import duties, non-refundable taxes, transport, handling, and directly attributable acquisition costs, net of trade discounts and rebates. 16. Q: How should damaged or obsolete inventory be measured at the reporting date?

17. Q: What is depreciation, and why is it different from an immediate expense on purchase?
A: Show answer
Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of a tangible asset over its useful life. It reflects the consumption of the asset’s service potential as the asset is used or as time passes. Charging the full cost of a long-lived machine in the purchase period would distort performance if the machine supports production over several years. Charging nothing after purchase would also overstate later-period performance and asset carrying amounts. Depreciation records an expense over the periods that benefit from use of the asset and reduces its carrying amount through accumulated depreciation. It is not a valuation exercise designed to show the asset’s market price, nor does it directly measure the cash needed to replace the asset.

Key point:
- Depreciation allocates cost over the periods benefiting from an asset’s use.
- It is not the same as a market-value revaluation or a cash reserve.

18. Q: How is straight-line depreciation calculated, including residual value?
A: Show answer
Under the straight-line method, the depreciable amount is allocated evenly over the asset’s useful life. The basic calculation is (cost less residual value) divided by useful life. If a machine costs 60,000, has an estimated residual value of zero, and a useful life of ten years, annual depreciation is 6,000. If an asset costs 200,000, is expected to be sold for 50,000 after 15 years, and has no other relevant adjustment, annual straight-line depreciation is 10,000. The estimate must reflect the period and pattern in which the entity expects to consume the asset’s benefits. Residual value and useful life are estimates and should be reviewed as required by the Standard.

Key point:
- Depreciable amount equals cost or revalued amount less residual value.
- Straight-line depreciation produces an equal annual charge over the useful life.

19. Q: Why are land and buildings often depreciated differently, and how should residual value be assessed?
A: Show answer
Land normally has an unlimited useful life and is therefore not depreciated, while a building usually has a finite useful life and is depreciated. Land improvements, such as roads, paving, or site works, may have finite lives and should be assessed separately. Residual value is the estimated amount the entity would currently obtain from disposal of the asset, after disposal costs, assuming it were already of the age and condition expected at the end of its useful life. It is not automatically zero and should not be confused with an expected future sale price without considering the relevant assumptions. In the chapter’s example, the building’s expected 50,000 residual value reduces its depreciable amount, while the land is not depreciated because its useful life is not finite.

20. Q: How should management select and review a depreciation method?
A: Show answer
The depreciation method should reflect the expected pattern in which the asset’s future economic benefits are consumed. Straight-line is appropriate when benefits are consumed evenly over time. A diminishing-balance method may be appropriate when benefits are consumed more heavily in earlier periods, and a units-of-production method may be suitable when wear depends on output or usage. 21. Q: What is impairment, and how does it differ from depreciation?
A: Show answer
Depreciation allocates an asset’s depreciable amount over its expected useful life based on normal consumption. Impairment recognizes an unexpected or additional reduction when the asset’s carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount. Damage, theft, technological obsolescence, adverse market changes, or a decision to restructure operations may indicate that an asset has lost value more quickly than expected. Depreciation continues even when there is no impairment indicator; impairment is an additional test required by IAS 36 when indicators exist and annually for specified assets such as goodwill and certain intangible assets. Inventory impairment is governed by IAS 2 rather than IAS 36. The two charges therefore address different causes and should not be treated as substitutes.

- Depreciation allocates expected consumption; impairment addresses a shortfall in recoverable amount.

22. Q: How is the recoverable amount of a non-financial asset determined under IAS 36?
A: Show answer
Recoverable amount is the higher of an asset’s fair value less costs of disposal and its value in use. Fair value less costs of disposal reflects a market-based disposal perspective after direct disposal costs. Value in use reflects the present value of future cash flows expected from continuing use and ultimate disposal. If carrying amount exceeds recoverable amount, the entity recognizes an impairment loss and reduces the carrying amount to recoverable amount. For example, if carrying amount is 500,000 and the two measures are 420,000 and 460,000, recoverable amount is 460,000 and the impairment loss is 40,000. The estimate requires supportable forecasts, appropriate discount assumptions, and disclosure where material judgments are significant.

24. Q: What makes an intangible asset identifiable under IAS 38?

26. Q: How does IAS 38 distinguish research expenditure from development expenditure?
A: Show answer

Development expenditure is capitalized only when the entity can demonstrate all specified criteria, including technical feasibility, intention and ability to complete and use or sell the asset, probable future economic benefits, adequate resources, and reliable measurement of attributable expenditure. Capitalization begins only when the criteria are met; earlier amounts already expensed are not reinstated later as an asset. 28. Q: What is a liability under the current IFRS Conceptual Framework?

30. Q: What is a provision, and how can it arise from a legal or constructive obligation?

32. Q: How are provisions measured, and why may a long-term provision be discounted?
A: Show answer

The estimate considers risks and uncertainties and should not be deliberately overstated or understated. 33. Q: How do provisions, contingent liabilities, and contingent assets differ?
A: Show answer
A provision is a recognized liability because a present obligation exists, an outflow is probable, and a reliable estimate can be made. A contingent liability is either a possible obligation whose existence depends on uncertain future events, or a present obligation that is not recognized because an outflow is not probable or cannot be measured reliably. It is disclosed unless the possibility of outflow is remote. A contingent asset is a possible asset dependent on uncertain future events outside the entity’s full control. It is not recognized while the inflow remains uncertain; disclosure is required when an inflow is probable, and recognition occurs when the inflow becomes virtually certain. This asymmetric treatment prevents speculative gains from being recorded prematurely while ensuring that material exposures are communicated.

5. Q: Why are historical costs usually irrelevant to a decision about future activity?

Historical costs are amounts already incurred. They are important for recording past transactions and measuring assets or expenses, but they cannot normally be changed by a new decision. The relevant information is the future cash flow, maintenance cost, resale value, alternative use, and capacity effect associated with each option. 11. Q: What are step-fixed costs, and how do they differ from ordinary fixed costs?

16. Q: What is contribution, and how does it connect sales to profit?

20. Q: How is net cash inflow calculated, and how does it relate to cash balances?

Analysis therefore helps managers direct investigation toward the relevant cause instead of responding to a total variance with an unfocused cost-cutting instruction.

32. Q: How is the material price variance calculated?
A: Show answer
The chapter gives the formula: material price variance equals actual price minus standard price, multiplied by actual quantity. In symbols, it is (Actual price − Standard price) × Actual quantity. If the actual price is below the standard, the result is favourable because the organization paid less per unit than expected. If the actual price is above the standard, it is unfavourable. The sign should be interpreted using the organization's favourable or adverse convention, and the calculation should use consistent units and a clearly defined quantity.

33. Q: How is the material usage variance calculated?
