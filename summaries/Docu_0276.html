4. Q: How should trade payables, accrued liabilities, provisions, commitments, advances, and prepayments be distinguished?
A: Show answer
Trade payables generally represent amounts owed to suppliers for goods or services received under an invoice or established billing arrangement. Accrued liabilities cover obligations arising from goods or services already received when the supplier invoice has not yet been processed or received. Provisions address obligations whose timing or amount is uncertain and require separate analysis under the applicable accounting framework. Commitments generally concern authorized future expenditure that has not necessarily resulted in a recognized liability. Advances and prepayments require assessment of whether a future benefit, recoverable balance, or other asset exists rather than assuming that the amount is an immediate expense or trade payable. The distinction depends on the underlying event, the existence of a present obligation, the reliability of measurement, and the applicable accounting criteria. Each classification has different implications for recognition, settlement, reversal, presentation, and disclosure. The AP register should not combine all these balances in one undifferentiated supplier-payable category. Uncertain cases should be referred to the relevant accounting specialist.

9. Q: How does three-way matching establish whether a purchase-order goods invoice is supported?
A: Show answer
Three-way matching compares the authorized purchase order, the supplier invoice, and evidence that the goods were received, inspected, and accepted. The review should confirm the legal entity, supplier, currency, ordered quantity, invoice quantity, accepted quantity, authorized price, delivery reference, prior billings, returns, credits, and remaining contractual or purchase-order ceiling. Receiving evidence establishes the relevant delivery or acceptance facts and should be provided by an appropriately authorized receiving function. Differences within a tolerance may be accepted only where the tolerance has been formally approved for the specific transaction type. Any unexplained excess quantity, rate variance, missing receipt, quality issue, or wrong-entity charge remains on hold until investigated and resolved.

14. Q: When should an expense or asset-related liability be recognized if the supplier invoice has not yet arrived?
A: Show answer
Under accrual-based accounting, recognition ordinarily follows the relevant event giving rise to the expense or asset, rather than the later arrival of the invoice or the payment date. The UN IPSAS Finance Manual's expense guidance explains the delivery principle: goods or services are recognized based on receipt or delivery, with the precise accounting treatment depending on the transaction and applicable requirements. When an organization has received qualifying goods or services before receiving the supplier's invoice, it may need to recognize the appropriate expense or asset and an accrued liability at the reporting date. The debit side must reflect the substance of the transaction; not every purchase is an immediate operating expense. An invoice received late must be assessed against the original service or delivery period and any accrual already recorded. The organization's accounting framework and policies govern the precise recognition criteria, measurement, journal, and presentation.

15. Q: How should the payable amount be calculated and independently verified?
A: Show answer
Establish a controlled calculation that separates line values, authorized freight and charges, approved discounts, verified tax, withholding, retention, linked credits, and authorized offsets. A typical calculation is: net value equals the total of line quantities multiplied by authorized unit prices, adjusted for authorized charges and discounts; gross value includes applicable verified taxes and adjustments; outstanding payable then reflects only supported and authorized deductions or linked credits. This formula is a calculation framework, not a substitute for jurisdiction-specific tax law or the supplier's contractual terms. Calculations should use appropriate decimal precision and approved currency-specific rounding, not rely on imprecise binary floating-point arithmetic for financial decisions. Independently recalculate material transactions and compare calculated results with the supplier invoice and approved system output. If a difference cannot be explained, retain the original figures, record the variance, and prevent posting until the issue is resolved or an authorized disposition is documented.
