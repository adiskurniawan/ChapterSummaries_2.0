1. Q: What does it mean to develop a digital mindset when working with machines?
A: Show answer
A digital mindset means understanding how digital systems operate and adapting one's behavior to their actual capabilities rather than assuming they think, understand, or respond like people. In this chapter, the central practical shift is to treat AI agents as machines even when they use human-like names, voices, or conversational interfaces. Users should give clear instructions, define the task, provide relevant context, and evaluate outputs according to the machine's demonstrated performance. The aim is neither to reject AI nor to trust it blindly, but to work with it deliberately and appropriately.
Study instruction:
- List three AI systems you use and write down what each is designed to do, what information it needs, and what it cannot reasonably be expected to infer.
Key point:
- Digital fluency includes knowing how to interact with machines on their own terms.
- Human-like presentation does not establish human-like understanding.

2. Q: What does the scheduling experience involving Professor Swanson and the AI agent Amy illustrate?
A: Show answer
The episode illustrates how people can misinterpret an AI agent's behavior when they assume that it understands social context in the same way a human assistant would. 4. Q: Why are the skills for working successfully with machines different from the skills for working successfully with people?
A: Show answer
Human interaction relies heavily on shared context, social conventions, tone, implied meaning, and the ability to interpret intentions. Many AI systems instead operate on data, programmed objectives, statistical patterns, and explicit inputs. A machine may not recognize that a request is socially delicate, that a preference was implied rather than stated, or that a phrase carries a particular nuance. Strategies that help with people, such as being more polite, waiting for a supposedly less busy moment, or hinting indirectly, may not improve a machine's output. Effective machine interaction therefore requires precise task framing, explicit constraints, and evaluation of the returned result.
Study instruction:
- Take one request normally made to a colleague and convert it into explicit instructions suitable for an automated system.
Key point:
- Social skill and machine-instruction skill are related but not interchangeable.
- Explicitness is often more useful than conversational subtlety.

6. Q: What is the difference between narrow AI and hypothetical general or superintelligent AI?
A: Show answer
The chapter focuses on narrow AI: systems designed to perform a specific task, such as playing chess, ranking search results, filtering spam, or predicting likely purchases. A system can perform its designated task extremely well without being able to transfer that ability to unrelated activities. General intelligence across a broad range of problems, and superintelligence that surpasses the best human capabilities across practically every field, are presented as different and more speculative concepts. A chess system that defeats expert players should not therefore be assumed to possess broad reasoning or social understanding. Users should evaluate an AI according to its actual task domain rather than extrapolating from impressive performance in one area.

9. Q: Why must data be cleaned before statistical models process them?
A: Show answer
Data collected from different sources may contain errors, missing values, duplicate records, inconsistent labels, or incompatible formats. Cleaning corrects or removes unsuitable information and standardizes values so that the system can interpret comparable records consistently. For example, one source might label a field “Population Data” while another uses “PopDat.” Unless the labels are reconciled, a system may treat them as different categories even when they represent the same concept. Poorly prepared data can distort patterns, classifications, and predictions. Data cleaning is therefore a substantive part of preparing an AI system, not merely a cosmetic formatting exercise.
Study instruction:
- Combine two small sample data tables and record duplicate entries, missing values, inconsistent labels, and format differences before standardizing them.
Key point:
- Inconsistent inputs can produce misleading model outputs.
- Data preparation should be documented and checked before analysis.

11. Q: What is machine learning?
A: Show answer
Machine learning is a form of AI in which algorithms use statistical methods to identify patterns in data and generate predictions or classifications. Rather than requiring a programmer to write a separate rule for every possible example, a learning system derives patterns from examples and can adjust its behavior as it receives additional data or feedback. 13. Q: Why does a machine-learning model need many varied training examples?
A: Show answer
A category can appear in many forms. A cat may be photographed from the front or side, in different lighting, at different distances, or against different backgrounds. If the training data contain only a narrow set of examples, a model may learn incidental characteristics rather than patterns that distinguish the category reliably. A varied dataset gives the model more opportunities to encounter meaningful differences and similarities. The chapter's examples therefore underline that the quantity of data matters, but variety and relevance matter as well. More examples do not automatically solve problems if the examples are inaccurate or unrepresentative.

Key point:
- Training data should represent the range of cases the model is expected to encounter.
- Volume without relevance or diversity can leave important gaps.

23. Q: What is unsupervised learning and how does it differ from supervised learning?
A: Show answer
Unsupervised learning looks for patterns or groupings in data without being given the same kind of preassigned outcome labels used in supervised learning. The chapter gives the example of an algorithm sorting through customer reviews or identifying items that are frequently purchased together. In supervised learning, the training examples include known categories or outcomes; in unsupervised learning, the system searches for structure in the data. A discovered grouping may be useful, but it still requires interpretation to determine what it means and whether it is relevant to a business decision. A statistical cluster should not automatically be treated as a meaningful real-world category.

Treating it as a completed project encourages leaders to declare success too early, stop learning, and become unprepared for the next disruption. A continuous-transition view makes adaptation, learning, and periodic redesign part of normal operations.
Study instruction:
- Identify a technology or process that has changed repeatedly and map the new skills, workflows, and decisions required at each stage.
Key point:
- Digital transformation has no durable end state because the environment continues to move.
- Organizations need repeatable capabilities for learning and redesign, not only project plans.
3. Q: How does a continuous digital transition differ from a conventional transitional state in change management?
A: Show answer
A conventional transitional state is usually described as a temporary in-between period during which an organization moves from familiar structures and norms to a new arrangement. People may experience uncertainty and strong emotions, but the model assumes that the transition will eventually be completed. In a digitally driven environment, the in-between condition recurs because tools, roles, processes, and customer needs keep evolving. Employees therefore cannot rely on reaching a final stable arrangement after a single change initiative. Leaders must help people repeatedly interpret what is changing, learn new practices, and let go of routines that no longer fit, while preserving enough continuity for the organization to function.
Study instruction:
- Compare a one-off restructuring with an ongoing digital program and identify which responsibilities continue after the formal implementation date.
Key point:
- Conventional transitions are bounded; digital transitions are recurrent.
- Leadership must support people through repeated periods of ambiguity and adjustment.
4. Q: What is the difference between merely adapting and becoming adaptive?
A: Show answer
Merely adapting can mean reacting to a particular change after it has occurred. Being adaptive means developing the ongoing ability to process new information, analyze what it implies, revise one's perspective, and prepare for the next wave of change. The chapter compares this to the way AI and machine-learning systems continually process data and refine their outputs. For people, the equivalent capability includes curiosity, willingness to question established assumptions, learning agility, and the ability to apply new knowledge to unfamiliar situations. An adaptive employee does not assume that mastering today's tool guarantees readiness for tomorrow's tool.
Study instruction:
- Choose a recent work change and distinguish the immediate reaction from the longer-term learning habits that would make you more adaptive next time.
Key point:
- Adaptation responds to a change; adaptivity is a continuing capability.
- Adaptive people use new information to prepare for further change.
5. Q: What does leadership mean when change is constant?

Leadership becomes the work of continually bringing coworkers, employees, partners, and customers through transitions. Leaders must explain why change is occurring, help people understand how their work will be affected, align processes and resources, and create conditions in which people can learn. They also need to monitor whether the intended change is helping users and customers rather than assuming that a technology rollout has achieved its purpose. This responsibility cannot be delegated entirely to an executive sponsor or an IT department because the transition affects everyday work, relationships, and decisions throughout the organization.
Study instruction:
- Draft a leader’s recurring transition responsibilities before, during, and after a digital tool or process is introduced.
Key point:
- Leading digital change is an ongoing people-and-process responsibility.
- Leaders must maintain alignment as technology and work practices evolve.
6. Q: Why can technology investment alone not create an organization’s digital mindset?

Software and infrastructure can provide capabilities, but they cannot by themselves make people comfortable with ambiguity, teach teams to coordinate across new boundaries, or establish the judgment needed to apply data in work. Employees still need to understand why a tool matters, learn how to use it, and adapt their roles and relationships. An organization may employ digital specialists or buy sophisticated platforms while leaving its established processes, incentives, and culture unchanged. The result can be a technically advanced unit that is disconnected from the rest of the business. A digital mindset must be developed by people and supported by organizational systems, leadership behavior, and learning opportunities.

- Technology enables transformation but does not supply the mindset or organizational alignment.

7. Q: What two areas must leaders manage together during a digital transition?
A: Show answer
Leaders must design and align systems and processes, while also preparing people for a digital organizational culture. The systems side includes workflows, team structures, information flows, tools, responsibilities, and coordination mechanisms. The people side includes skills, attitudes, expectations, confidence, identity, and willingness to learn. These areas influence one another: a new team design changes what employees need to know, while employees’ capabilities and behavior affect whether the new design works. Managing only one side creates predictable problems, such as efficient digital tools that do not connect to existing channels or a motivated workforce constrained by outdated processes.
Study instruction:
- For a proposed transformation, create two linked workstreams—systems and people—and show the dependencies between their milestones.
Key point:
- Process redesign and culture change are interdependent.

8. Q: Why did the appliance manufacturer’s first digital transformation attempt fail despite a successful new digital unit?

The new unit initially delivered speed and innovation, but its online channel was not connected to traditional face-to-face channels. Customers and partners experienced fragmented service, channel revenue declined, and tensions between departments increased. Internal communication then broke down and the digital unit collapsed. The failure was not evidence that the technology itself was ineffective; the organization had failed to create a transition process that connected the new capability to existing operations and prepared the wider workforce to work differently.

- A digital island can succeed locally while damaging the wider organization.

9. Q: What changed in the appliance manufacturer’s second transformation attempt?

The company appointed an internal leader who understood the organization and was respected across departments for perseverance, willingness to learn, and adaptability. She introduced training that guided employees from sales staff to executives through each stage of the initiative, helping them understand its potential rather than fear unfamiliar practices. She also reconnected with customers individually, learned their specific needs, and adjusted the digital business model accordingly. This approach developed internal understanding and connected the transformation to real customer requirements. The lesson is that a credible internal change leader can bridge organizational history, employee concerns, and new digital ambitions more effectively than a detached technical solution alone.

- Compare the first and second approaches and identify the differences in sponsorship, workforce preparation, customer engagement, and cross-department coordination.

- Successful transformation requires an organizational transition process, not just a new unit.

10. Q: What does it mean to design agile, autonomous, and collaborative systems for digital work?

It means structuring work so that teams can respond quickly to customer or operational needs without waiting for multiple disconnected handovers. Cross-functional teams bring together the capabilities required to address an outcome, while autonomy gives them room to make appropriate day-to-day decisions. Collaboration and transparent communication help the team coordinate dependencies and learn from results. Autonomy does not mean absence of accountability: teams still need clear goals, decision rights, performance measures, and escalation routes. Leaders should remove unnecessary bureaucracy while retaining controls that protect quality, security, compliance, and organizational coherence.

- Agile structures shorten coordination loops and bring related expertise together.

11. Q: What can ING’s use of agile tribes teach organizations about customer-centered redesign?

The organization sought to make the customer experience itself central rather than treating banking only as a service, a shift described as “un-banking the bank.” The example shows that digital transformation may require redesigning ownership and team structure before technology can deliver a coherent customer experience. 12. Q: How did Unilever combine global scale with responsiveness to local markets?

Unilever used globally distributed agile teams that could focus on the needs of particular local markets while drawing on the company’s digital capabilities across countries. Its approach had three connected dimensions: enabling technologies that reduced global-local divides, redesigned processes that used those tools effectively, and people who could make the change work. The company formed hundreds of small teams and deployed thousands of team members worldwide. The lesson is not simply to create remote teams; it is to connect local market knowledge with shared platforms, common processes, and people who can translate data into relevant local action. Global consistency and local adaptation must be designed together.

- Digital platforms can connect global capabilities with local responsiveness.

13. Q: Why is integrating data and communication across organizational silos so important?

Siloed departments often hold information that is useful to other teams but cannot be accessed or acted on quickly. When data flows across functions, teams can coordinate in real time, see dependencies, combine expertise, and make decisions using a more complete picture. Sophisticated algorithms or tools are less valuable when the organization cannot connect the people, data, and decisions needed to use them. Integration therefore concerns governance, workflow, roles, and collaboration as much as technical interfaces.

- Integrated information flows enable coordinated work and faster learning.

14. Q: What is the lesson of Moderna’s approach to digitization and operational excellence?
A: Show answer
Moderna treated data access, cloud capability, and automation as mutually reinforcing elements of its operating model. Teams worked with shared data rather than remaining isolated in traditional functional silos, and digital and operational excellence were connected in leadership responsibility. The chapter emphasizes that process design must precede digitization: automating a poor analog process can simply reproduce its weaknesses at greater speed or scale. Moderna’s integrated structure made it easier to reuse existing digital solutions and develop specialized algorithms when needed. The wider lesson is to improve the work and its interfaces before automating it, then organize teams so that the resulting information can be used across functions.

15. Q: How should managers and IT departments divide responsibilities when selecting cloud-based digital tools?
A: Show answer
IT remains important for enterprise systems, bespoke implementations, security, cost, sustainability, and the technical environment. However, many cloud-based software-as-a-service tools can be licensed and deployed at departmental or project level without a large technical implementation. Managers understand the work, customer outcomes, roles, and relationships that the tool is expected to change; they therefore need to lead the business case, process redesign, user adoption, and operational implementation. IT should provide appropriate governance and technical advice rather than being excluded or expected to own every organizational decision. Clear decision rights prevent uncontrolled departmental purchases while keeping implementation close to the people who understand the work.

16. Q: Why should IT be consulted even when a department can deploy a SaaS tool independently?
A: Show answer
A simple cloud deployment can still create organization-wide consequences. Multiple subscriptions may duplicate costs, expose confidential information, create inconsistent access controls, complicate integrations, or introduce sustainability and vendor-management concerns. A tool may also store data in ways that conflict with organizational requirements or create dependencies that are difficult to unwind. Involving IT and other relevant control functions early allows managers to assess security, architecture, interoperability, data handling, support, and lifecycle costs before users become dependent on the service. The objective is not to slow every small experiment with excessive bureaucracy, but to establish risk-based review proportionate to the tool and data involved.

Key point:
- Ease of deployment does not remove security, cost, or sustainability risks.
- Early technical review can preserve flexibility while preventing unmanaged technology sprawl.
17. Q: What is a bold stroke in a digital transformation?
A: Show answer
A bold stroke is a visible action that commands attention and signals that the organization must move in a new direction. Examples in the chapter include a major reorganization, an acquisition, a significant diversion of resources, appointing a digital transformation leader who reports to the CEO, closing legacy systems, or requiring employees to learn a technical skill within a defined period. Such an action can disrupt complacency and make the strategic priority unmistakable. Its value lies in signaling seriousness and creating momentum, not in guaranteeing adoption. A bold stroke can provoke anxiety or resistance if people do not understand the rationale or receive the support needed to act on it.

- Evaluate a proposed bold stroke by identifying the message it sends, the groups it affects, and the support required after the announcement.

- A bold stroke makes the need for a new direction visible.

18. Q: What is the “long march” that must follow a bold stroke?

The long march is the organized, sustained advancement toward new routines and behaviors after the initial signal of change. It includes communicating the rationale repeatedly, redesigning processes, building skills, addressing resistance, reinforcing new expectations, and helping teams apply the new practices in everyday work. Without this sustained effort, the organization may experience a short burst of attention followed by a return to familiar routines. Leaders need to translate the broad direction into milestones, responsibilities, learning support, and feedback loops. The long march is where a transformation becomes operational rather than remaining a slogan or executive announcement.

- Turn a digital transformation announcement into a six-month sequence of practical changes, owners, learning activities, and review points.

- Bold strokes create attention; sustained routines create durable change.
- Transformation needs reinforcement and follow-through after launch.
19. Q: Why must leaders address organizational culture during digital transformation?

Culture consists of shared values, norms, attitudes, and behaviors that shape how people interpret change and act at work. A new tool may require employees to share information, experiment, collaborate across departments, or make decisions differently. If the existing culture rewards information hoarding, punishes mistakes, or values functional control over customer outcomes, employees may resist those behaviors even when the technology is available. Leaders must therefore explain the desired identity and norms, model them, and align incentives and management practices with the new way of working. Culture changes through repeated experience and reinforcement, not through slogans alone.

- Culture shapes whether people use digital tools in the intended way.

20. Q: What two questions form the chapter’s employee adoption framework?
A: Show answer
The framework asks whether a person has bought into the transformation and whether the person believes they can learn what is needed to succeed in the transformed organization. Buy-in concerns the perceived value and legitimacy of the change for the employee and the organization. Capability confidence concerns whether the person believes they can acquire and apply the required skills. These are separate judgments: an employee may support the strategic goal but doubt their ability to learn, or may possess strong technical skills but see no reason to change. Managers should diagnose both dimensions rather than treating all reluctance as the same problem.
Study instruction:
- Interview a sample of users and record their answers separately for perceived value and confidence in learning the required skills.
Key point:
- Adoption depends on both motivation to change and confidence in learning.
- A single generic change message will not address every employee concern.
21. Q: How should managers interpret the four employee responses in the adoption framework?
A: Show answer
The framework combines two dimensions—buy-in and perceived ability to learn—into four broad responses. Employees who see value and believe they can learn are inspired and are the most ready to engage. Those who see value but doubt their ability may feel frustrated and need confidence-building support. Those who feel capable but do not see a reason to change may be indifferent and need a clearer explanation of the purpose and personal or organizational benefit. Those who lack both buy-in and confidence may feel oppressed or overwhelmed and need both a credible rationale and practical learning support. The labels are diagnostic prompts, not permanent identities; an individual’s position can change with experience and circumstances.
Study instruction:
- Place four fictional employees in the matrix and design a different management response for each combination of buy-in and confidence.
Key point:
- Diagnose the reason for reluctance before choosing an intervention.
- Employees can move between response patterns as understanding and capability change.
22. Q: How can leaders increase employee buy-in for digital transformation?

Effective messaging connects the change to meaningful goals rather than relying on abstract statements about modernization. Internal campaigns should use credible examples of how work, customer outcomes, or professional opportunities can improve. Identity matters because people are more likely to engage when they can see a valued role for themselves in the future organization.

23. Q: How can managers build employees’ confidence that they can learn new digital skills?

The chapter identifies past experience, vicarious experience, and persuasion or encouragement as important sources of confidence. Past experience includes earlier learning or successful use of technology in education, work, or daily life. Vicarious experience comes from seeing peers and managers learn and apply unfamiliar skills, which makes success appear attainable. Encouragement from managers and executives can reinforce confidence, especially when it is specific and accompanied by access to training, time, feedback, and practice. Encouragement without resources is unlikely to be persuasive. Managers should create achievable learning steps and visible examples of colleagues progressing from beginner to competent user.

Key point:
- Confidence can be strengthened through mastery, role models, and credible encouragement.

24. Q: Why might employees reject a digital tool even when leaders have announced that it is mandatory?
A: Show answer
Employees judge whether the tool helps them carry out the goals announced by leadership in the context of their actual work. At Autoworks, roughly 40 percent of potential users did not adopt the new design software, even when supervisors required it. Some experienced engineers believed the new software made their own work slower than familiar legacy tools, so they saw no personal reason to switch. Their rejection also influenced respected colleagues, creating negative peer effects. A mandate may produce nominal compliance but cannot by itself create understanding, confidence, consistent use, or perceived value. Managers need to investigate local experience and remove practical barriers rather than assuming resistance is simply stubbornness.

- Conduct user interviews that ask what task the tool is meant to improve, what users experience in practice, and what trade-offs they see compared with existing tools.

- Employees evaluate technology through the work they personally need to perform.
- Mandates do not replace credible value, usability, and support.
25. Q: What does the Autoworks example teach about the language leaders use to promote a digital tool?

Autoworks leaders repeatedly described the goal as making product development “faster and cheaper.” They intended this as an enterprise-level objective, but engineers interpreted it as a direct comparison between the new software and the tools they already knew. Some concluded that the new tool slowed their individual tasks, even though it offered other organizational advantages. Leaders had not translated the broad ambition into benefits meaningful to different roles. Communication should therefore explain how the transformation improves outcomes that matter to each user group, such as design quality, risk detection, customer response, or reduced rework, while being honest about temporary learning costs and trade-offs.

- Rewrite a generic transformation slogan into role-specific explanations for technical users, managers, sales staff, and customers.

27. Q: What six practices support effective employee learning programs for digital transformation?

Existing employees already possess institutional knowledge, customer understanding, process experience, and relationships that new hires need time to develop. Digital hard skills can often be learned, while organization-specific knowledge is accumulated through experience. A balanced approach may still recruit specialists where needed, but it should also create pathways for current employees to acquire the skills required for evolving roles. 30. Q: What is the six-phase work digitization process described in the chapter?
A: Show answer
The work digitization process, or WDP, describes how a digital transformation is experienced in everyday work: (1) leaders sell the transformation; (2) employees decide whether to use the new technology; (3) employees decide how to use it; (4) new kinds of data change employee behavior; (5) performance improves locally; and (6) local performance aligns with company goals. The phases show why announcing a tool and training users are not sufficient. Adoption choices, usage patterns, data quality, changing roles, local benefits, and strategic alignment all influence whether the initiative creates lasting value. The process is useful as a diagnostic model because a transformation can stall at any phase.
Study instruction:
- Apply all six WDP phases to a digital initiative and identify evidence that would show whether each phase has been achieved.
Key point:
- Transformation progresses through linked decisions and behavioral changes, not just technical deployment.
- Each phase can enable or undermine the phases that follow.

32. Q: What should managers learn from employees’ decision whether to use a new technology?

33. Q: Why does the way employees use a digital tool matter as much as whether they adopt it?
A: Show answer
A digital tool can be used in many ways, and the features and sequence employees choose determine what data are generated and how comparable those data will be. At Autoworks, two departments used the same simulation tool, but one allowed highly varied individual practices while the other used the same features in the same order. The standardized group generated data with a more uniform foundation, making patterns easier to compare and learn from. The chapter reports substantially stronger vehicle performance in that group. Standardization should focus on the practices necessary for meaningful comparison and quality; it should not suppress legitimate experimentation where different methods are needed and can be documented.

Key point:
- Consistent usage can make data more comparable and useful for learning.
- Adoption without suitable usage practices may produce large volumes of weakly comparable data.

The appropriate tool and process redesign follow from that analysis rather than being chosen because a technology is fashionable or already available. During implementation, the organization continues gathering data to test whether the effort is working and whether employee behavior is helping or hindering progress.
