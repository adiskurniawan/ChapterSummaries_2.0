1. Q: What is the central argument of Chapter 1, “Decision Making Under Uncertainty and Problem Solving”?
A: Show answer
The chapter argues that people must often choose and act even when information about events, probabilities, consequences, or available options is incomplete. Uncertainty is not a single condition: it can arise from randomness, ambiguity, missing knowledge, delayed outcomes, ethical questions, and changes in learned patterns. It influences cognition, emotion, risk perception, and behavior. The chapter therefore recommends understanding the type and source of uncertainty before selecting a response. Its practical conclusion is not that uncertainty can always be eliminated, but that decision makers can manage it through information gathering, acknowledgement, flexibility, precaution, and repeated reassessment.

2. Q: What is uncertainty in the context of decision making?
A: Show answer
Uncertainty describes a condition in which the decision maker lacks complete knowledge about the situation, the possible outcomes, their likelihood, or their consequences. It may result from incomplete information, randomness, ignorance, ambiguity, or changes in the environment. Even when some facts are known, the outcome may remain uncertain because events are probabilistic. Uncertainty is therefore broader than simply not knowing what will happen: it also concerns how complete, reliable, and interpretable the available knowledge is. The chapter treats uncertainty as something that affects how people judge options and decide what action to take.

3. Q: Why is subjective uncertainty important for understanding judgement and choice?
A: Show answer
Subjective uncertainty concerns how a person experiences the incompleteness of knowledge in a particular situation. Two people may face the same objective facts but interpret the amount of uncertainty differently because they have different experience, information, motivations, confidence, or tolerance for ambiguity. Those appraisals can influence whether they seek more information, avoid the choice, rely on intuition, or act despite incomplete knowledge. The chapter emphasizes that decision making cannot be explained only by calculating probabilities; the decision maker's perception and emotional response also matter. A practical analysis should therefore examine both the external situation and the person's understanding of it.

4. Q: What are the three traditional approaches to understanding how people handle uncertainty?
A: Show answer
The chapter describes the knowledge seeker, certainty maximizer, and intuitive statistician-economist perspectives. The knowledge seeker is motivated by novel information and experience and may tolerate ignorance while learning. The certainty maximizer is more concerned about the disruptive effects of unpredictability and may seek to reduce the anxiety associated with uncertainty. The intuitive statistician-economist perspective emphasizes rational choice, probability, cognition, and information processing, treating people as decision makers who seek desirable outcomes and avoid undesirable ones. These are broad explanatory traditions rather than fixed personality types; one person may use different approaches in different situations.
Study instruction:
- "Classify a recent decision in terms of information seeking, certainty seeking, and probability-based evaluation."
Key point:
- People can respond to uncertainty through learning, certainty seeking, or analytical evaluation.

5. Q: How can uncertainty be understood through probability, delay, and lack of clarity?
A: Show answer
The chapter, drawing on Smithson, describes uncertainty through three related constructs: probability or randomness, delay in consequences, and absence or lack of clarity. Probability concerns the likelihood of different outcomes. Delay means that consequences are not immediate, making it harder to judge what an action will produce. Lack of clarity includes vagueness, ambiguity, missing information, and incomplete understanding of the situation. These dimensions can occur together. For example, a project may have uncertain success probabilities, benefits that will appear only after several years, and unclear evidence about the assumptions behind its forecast. Identifying the dimension helps determine what information or safeguards are useful.
Study instruction:
- "Take a complex decision and assess its probability, timing, and clarity uncertainties separately."
Key point:
- Uncertainty can concern likelihood, timing, or the clarity of what is known.
- Do not treat all uncertainty as a single problem with one solution.

6. Q: What is the difference between uncertainty, ambiguity, vagueness, and ignorance?
A: Show answer
These terms describe related but distinct forms of incomplete knowledge. Uncertainty is the broad condition in which the decision maker cannot fully determine the situation or outcome. Ambiguity concerns unclear or multiple possible interpretations, including cases where probabilities themselves are not known. Vagueness concerns imprecise boundaries or descriptions, such as an undefined standard of acceptable performance. Ignorance refers to knowledge that is absent or not recognized; the chapter discusses forms such as distortion and incompleteness. The distinctions matter because more data may reduce a knowledge gap but may not resolve conflicting interpretations, unclear definitions, or unknown possibilities.

7. Q: What does the continuum from certainty to pure uncertainty represent?
A: Show answer
The chapter presents a continuum with deterministic certainty at one pole and pure uncertainty at the other, with risk-related situations between them. At the deterministic end, the decision maker has complete knowledge of the situation and the outcome is effectively certain or impossible. Moving away from that pole, available knowledge becomes less complete and outcomes become less predictable. Pure uncertainty represents profound ignorance in which the decision maker lacks a meaningful basis for assigning probabilities. The continuum is a conceptual aid rather than a precise measurement scale. It helps show that decisions differ in how much is known and how confidently outcomes can be estimated.

9. Q: How does the chapter distinguish risk from uncertainty?
A: Show answer
Risk is described as the likelihood or probability of an outcome, while uncertainty concerns the quality and completeness of the knowledge used to assess that risk. In a risky decision, the decision maker may be able to estimate the probabilities of outcomes. In a decision under uncertainty, the probabilities themselves may be unknown or difficult to estimate. The chapter also notes that risk and uncertainty are closely related and that some writers use risk broadly to include situations with both known and unknown probabilities. In practice, it is important to state whether probabilities are measured, estimated, disputed, or unavailable.

10. Q: What is Knightian uncertainty, and how does it differ from measurable risk?
A: Show answer
Knight distinguished measurable risk from uncertainty that cannot be meaningfully quantified. Under measurable risk, the decision maker can assign probabilities using data, models, or a defensible frequency distribution. Under Knightian uncertainty, the relevant possibilities or their probabilities are not known well enough to support a reliable numerical estimate. This distinction matters because applying a precise probability model to a situation with no defensible basis can create false precision. A decision maker may need scenarios, safeguards, flexible commitments, or staged decisions instead of relying on expected-value calculations alone.
Study instruction:
- "Identify a decision where historical data supports probability estimates and another where the possible outcomes or probabilities are not adequately known."
Key point:
- Not every unknown can be converted into a reliable probability.
- Do not disguise Knightian uncertainty with unsupported numerical precision.

14. Q: What does prospect theory contribute to understanding decisions under risk?
A: Show answer
The chapter discusses prospect theory as a challenge to models that assume people consistently maximize expected utility. Prospect theory emphasizes that people evaluate outcomes in relation to perceived gains and losses and may show both risk aversion and risk seeking depending on how a choice is framed. A person may prefer a certain gain over a gamble with a similar expected value, yet become more willing to gamble when facing losses. The theory helps explain why preferences can change with reference points and presentation. It does not imply that people always behave irrationally; it shows that actual choice is influenced by psychological evaluation as well as numerical outcomes.
Study instruction:

Key point:

18. Q: What is first-order uncertainty?
A: Show answer
First-order uncertainty concerns variation in the outcomes themselves. A decision maker may know the set of possible outcomes but not know which one will occur. For example, a project may have several possible delivery dates or financial results, each depending on uncertain events. The uncertainty lies in which outcome will be realized. Statistical models may represent this variation through probability distributions when sufficient information exists. However, first-order uncertainty does not automatically mean that the probabilities are known with confidence; the decision maker must separately assess the reliability of the model and the evidence behind it.

19. Q: What is second-order uncertainty, and why is it different from first-order uncertainty?
A: Show answer
Second-order uncertainty concerns imprecision about the probabilities or boundaries used to describe first-order outcomes. The decision maker may know that several outcomes are possible but be unsure whether the estimated probability distribution is correct. For example, a forecast may assign a 30 percent chance to a delay, while the analyst is also uncertain whether 30 percent is a defensible estimate. Second-order uncertainty therefore concerns uncertainty about the uncertainty model itself. It matters because a decision based on a precise-looking distribution can be fragile if the underlying probabilities are poorly supported.
Study instruction:
- "Take a forecast and distinguish uncertainty about what will happen from uncertainty about the accuracy of its probability estimates."
Key point:
- Second-order uncertainty is uncertainty about the estimates or boundaries used to model outcomes.
- Assess model confidence separately from outcome variability.

20. Q: How should a decision maker distinguish epistemological and ontological uncertainty?
A: Show answer
Epistemological uncertainty arises from gaps in knowledge that could potentially be reduced through better information, observation, or research. The decision maker may need to investigate, verify assumptions, or obtain missing evidence. Ontological uncertainty arises from the complex or evolving nature of the system itself, including social, biological, or technical interactions that cannot be fully captured by deterministic reasoning. More information may help, but it cannot necessarily eliminate the uncertainty. The distinction is practical: knowledge gaps may call for research, while system complexity may require adaptive decisions, monitoring, and flexibility.

Key point:
- Some uncertainty comes from what we do not know; some comes from how complex the world is.
- Use research for reducible knowledge gaps and adaptive safeguards for irreducible complexity.

25. Q: What is the stimulus-response-outcome (S-R-O) model of learned certainty?
A: Show answer
The S-R-O model describes how people learn associations between a stimulus, a response, and the outcome that follows. If a particular response to a situation repeatedly produces a positive result, the association becomes stronger and may guide future choices. For example, a shopper may return to a store because earlier visits produced satisfactory purchases. This learning can make future decisions feel more predictable. However, a strong association can become unreliable when the environment changes. Decision makers should use experience as evidence while remaining alert to new conditions that may invalidate familiar patterns.
Study instruction:
- "Identify a learned S-R-O pattern in your own behavior and note what change would make the association unreliable."
Key point:
- Past outcomes shape expectations about future choices.
- Use experience as a guide, but test whether the conditions remain comparable.

26. Q: What is expected uncertainty in the S-R-O framework?
A: Show answer
Expected uncertainty occurs when previously learned stimulus-response-outcome associations are no longer reliable indicators of the result, but the instability is known and relatively consistent. The decision maker understands that the old pattern cannot predict the outcome with the same confidence. Because the uncertainty is anticipated, it can be incorporated into planning and monitoring. For example, a seasonal process may regularly produce variable results, so the decision maker can build that variability into schedules or reserves. Expected uncertainty differs from a sudden unexpected change because the need to refresh assumptions is already recognized.

- Expected uncertainty is known instability in the relationship between action and outcome.
