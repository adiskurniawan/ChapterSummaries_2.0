Q&A General Knowledge
1. Q: How would you support program managers and project leaders who are responsible for financial performance but are not finance specialists?
A: Show answer
The Finance Manager should make financial information usable for operational decision-making rather than expecting program managers to become accountants. Support should combine clear management reports, regular budget reviews, variance explanations, forecasting assistance, guidance on expenditure controls, and timely escalation of risks. The finance team should explain what the numbers mean for activities, deliverables, staffing, procurement, and donor commitments. Program managers should remain accountable for their budgets and operational choices, while Finance provides the technical interpretation, challenge, tools, and assurance. A practical monthly review can focus on budget, actual expenditure, commitments, forecast, major variances, available funding, upcoming obligations, and actions required. This creates a partnership in which Finance acts as a decision-support function and the program team retains ownership of program delivery and resource use.

Study instruction:
- "Learn how Finance can translate accounting information into practical program-management decisions without transferring technical accounting responsibility to program staff."
Key point:
- Program managers need understandable financial information, not accounting theory.
- Finance should provide analysis, challenge, tools, and assurance while managers retain operational accountability.

2. Q: Why is financial support for non-finance program managers important?
A: Show answer
Program decisions almost always have financial consequences, even when the manager’s primary responsibility is technical delivery. A change in activity timing can alter cash requirements, a procurement decision can affect the remaining budget, delayed implementation can create an underspend, and an acceleration of activities can create pressure on funding or procurement capacity. Non-finance managers therefore need enough financial understanding to recognize these consequences early. Financial support reduces the risk that a program appears operationally on track while its funding position, expenditure profile, commitments, or compliance position is deteriorating. Effective support also creates shared accountability because program and Finance teams review the same facts and discuss corrective actions together.

Study instruction:
- "Understand why financial performance is part of program management rather than a separate Finance-only responsibility."
Key point:
- Operational decisions create financial consequences.
- Early finance involvement allows problems to be addressed before they become material.
Source links:
- UNDP — Monitor: Programme and Project Performance Monitoring
- National Council of Nonprofits — Financial Management
3. Q: What financial information should program managers understand?
A: Show answer
A program manager does not necessarily need to understand every general-ledger account, journal type, or accounting standard, but should understand the financial information needed to control a project. At a minimum, this includes the approved budget, actual expenditure, commitments or obligations, available balance, forecast expenditure, major variances, funding restrictions, relevant cost categories, and significant financial risks. Managers should also understand the difference between cash paid, expenditure recorded, and commitments that may become expenditure later. Where grants are involved, they should know key funding dates, reporting deadlines, eligible-cost rules, reallocation restrictions, and the consequences of underspending or overspending. The information should be presented in a way that connects directly to activities and outputs.

Study instruction:
- "Build a basic financial vocabulary around budget, actuals, commitments, forecast, variance, available balance, and restricted funding."
Key point:
- Managers need the financial concepts that influence their decisions.
- Reports should connect financial information to activities and results.

- UNDP — Project Finance Officer, Project Budget Management and Reporting
4. Q: How would you explain a budget to a program manager who is uncomfortable with financial terminology?
A: Show answer
I would explain the budget as the financial expression of the approved work plan. Instead of starting with accounting terminology, I would start with planned activities, expected quantities, timing, unit costs, staffing, procurement, travel, partner costs, and other resources needed to deliver the program. Each budget line should answer a practical question: what activity is this funding intended to support, when will the cost occur, and what will happen if the cost changes? The manager should then see the budget as a planning tool rather than a Finance document. Once that foundation is clear, Finance can introduce terms such as variance and forecast using the manager’s own project as the example. This approach makes financial responsibility easier to understand because the numbers are linked to work the manager already knows.

Study instruction:
- "Practice explaining budgets through activities, quantities, timing, and resources before introducing technical accounting language."
Key point:
- A project budget is a financial representation of the work plan.
- Practical examples are often more effective than accounting terminology alone.
Source links:
- National Council of Nonprofits — Building a Better Budget
- UNICEF — Summary of Budget Management for Implementing Partners
5. Q: How would you explain budget versus actual results to a non-finance manager?
A: Show answer
I would begin by explaining that budget versus actual compares what was planned with what has actually been recorded for the same period. I would then separate the comparison into three questions: what was supposed to happen, what actually happened, and why the difference exists. For example, if a training budget was $20,000 for the quarter but actual expenditure is $12,000, the $8,000 difference is not automatically a problem. Activities may have been delayed, invoices may not yet have arrived, procurement may still be underway, or the activity may genuinely cost less than expected. The next step is therefore explanation and forecast, not automatic correction. Managers should also understand cumulative results and the expected year-end position so that a temporary timing variance is not confused with a structural underspend.

Study instruction:
- "Review budget-versus-actual reports by asking what was planned, what happened, why the difference exists, and what is expected next."
Key point:
- A variance is a signal for analysis, not an automatic error.
- Timing, operational activity, and genuine cost changes must be distinguished.

- National Council of Nonprofits — Financial Reports and Variances
- UNICEF — Budget Monitoring and Financial Analysis
6. Q: How would you identify whether a budget variance requires management action?
A: Show answer
I would not rely on a single percentage threshold. I would consider the size of the variance, the remaining project period, the nature of the budget line, the donor rules, whether the variance is caused by timing, whether it affects key outputs, and whether it changes the forecast. A small percentage variance on a large critical procurement can matter more than a larger percentage variance on a very small line. A recurring monthly deviation may also be more significant than a one-off timing difference. The review should therefore classify variances as timing-related, volume-related, price-related, coding-related, scope-related, or potentially compliance-related. The management response should be proportionate: monitor, investigate, reforecast, obtain approval, revise the work plan, or escalate as appropriate.

Study instruction:
- "Assess variance significance using amount, percentage, cause, timing, program effect, and donor implications rather than one threshold alone."
Key point:
- Materiality depends on context as well as amount.
- The cause and consequence of a variance determine the appropriate response.

7. Q: How would you help a program manager distinguish a timing variance from a real financial problem?
A: Show answer
I would trace the variance back to the implementation timetable and supporting transactions. Suppose a manager expected $50,000 of procurement costs in March but only $10,000 has been recorded. The difference could simply mean delivery will occur in April. Finance should check purchase orders, contracts, commitments, delivery dates, invoices, payment status, and implementation milestones before concluding that the program is underspending. A timing variance becomes more concerning when the underlying activity is also delayed or when procurement has not started. The forecast should then be updated so the manager can see whether the cost is merely moving between months or whether the annual spending plan itself has changed.

Study instruction:
- "Use procurement status, commitments, delivery dates, and implementation milestones to test whether a variance is timing-related."
Key point:
- Timing differences should be tested against operational evidence.
- A delayed transaction and a delayed activity are related but not necessarily the same problem.

8. Q: How would you help program managers forecast future expenditure?
A: Show answer
I would start from the remaining work plan rather than simply extrapolating historical spending. The manager should identify activities still to be delivered, procurement still to be completed, contracts remaining, staffing requirements, partner transfers, travel, and other known obligations. Finance can then combine this operational information with actual expenditure, commitments, open purchase orders, historical spending patterns, and current cost assumptions. The forecast should distinguish committed costs from planned but not yet committed costs. Where uncertainty is significant, Finance can prepare a base case and one or two alternative scenarios. Forecasting should be refreshed regularly because it is a management estimate, not a one-time annual exercise.

Study instruction:
- "Build forecasts from remaining activities and known obligations, then reconcile them to actuals and commitments."
Key point:
- Forecasts should be forward-looking and activity-based.
- Good forecasting combines operational information with financial data.

9. Q: What should a monthly financial review with a program manager look like?
A: Show answer
The meeting should be short, structured, and decision-focused. A useful agenda is: current expenditure against budget; major variances; commitments and upcoming obligations; forecast to project end; funding remaining; key control or compliance issues; and agreed actions. Finance should prepare the report before the meeting and highlight only the items requiring discussion rather than reading every line. The program manager should explain operational causes, confirm whether planned activities remain realistic, and identify decisions needed. Each meeting should end with named actions, responsible persons, and deadlines. The purpose is not to turn the manager into an accountant but to make financial performance part of routine project management.

Study instruction:
- "Use a repeatable monthly agenda that turns financial reporting into an action and decision forum."
Key point:
- Monthly reviews should focus on decisions and exceptions.
- Every identified issue should have an owner and follow-up date.

10. Q: How would you decide what financial information each program manager actually needs?
A: Show answer
The report should be designed around the manager’s decisions, not around everything Finance happens to have available. A project manager responsible for field implementation may need activity-level budgets, procurement commitments, travel costs, partner balances, and forecast delivery. A country program manager may also need donor-level funding, co-financing, staffing allocations, and overall portfolio risks. I would interview managers about their recurring decisions, identify the financial information needed for those decisions, and then build a standardized report with role-specific views. The underlying data can remain consistent while the presentation changes by responsibility. This prevents information overload and increases the chance that managers actually use the report.

Study instruction:
- "Design management reports around decisions and responsibilities rather than providing every available financial field."
Key point:
- Different responsibilities require different views of the same financial data.
- Relevance is more useful than volume.

11. Q: How would you explain commitments to a non-finance project leader?
A: Show answer
I would explain a commitment as a financial obligation that has been created even though the cash may not yet have been paid. For example, if a project signs a valid purchase order for equipment, the organization may have a future cost even before the supplier sends an invoice. Looking only at paid expenditure can therefore make a project appear to have more available budget than it truly has. Program managers should review commitments alongside actual expenditure when deciding whether additional spending is affordable. Finance should make clear which commitments are firm, which are estimates, and which have been cancelled or completed, because stale commitments can also distort the available-balance calculation.

Study instruction:
- "Distinguish actual expenditure from commitments so the project’s remaining spending capacity is not overstated."
Key point:
- Cash paid is not the same as total financial exposure.
- Commitments provide important forward-looking information.

12. Q: How would you explain financial accountability without making program managers responsible for technical accounting?
A: Show answer
Accountability should be separated from technical responsibility. A program manager can be accountable for using resources according to the approved plan, obtaining the required approvals, confirming that activities occurred, monitoring budget performance, and escalating significant changes. Finance remains responsible for accounting treatment, financial reporting methodology, reconciliations, technical compliance interpretation, and quality assurance. The two roles should be documented so there is no ambiguity about who owns each control. This makes accountability fair: managers are not expected to determine complex accounting rules, but they are expected to provide accurate operational information and take action when their program’s financial position requires it.

Study instruction:
- "Map operational accountability and technical accounting responsibility separately, then define how the two functions interact."
Key point:
- Accountability does not require everyone to perform the same technical role.
- Clear role definitions prevent gaps and duplicated responsibilities.

- UNDP — Project Finance Roles and Responsibilities
- UNDP — Annual Performance Planning and Financial Management Accountability
13. Q: What financial training would be most useful for non-finance program managers?
A: Show answer
Training should be practical rather than accounting-heavy. Core topics should include how a budget is built, how to read budget-versus-actual reports, how to explain variances, how commitments affect available budget, how to forecast, what supporting documentation is required, what expenditure requires prior approval, how restricted funding works, and when an issue should be escalated to Finance. Short scenario-based sessions are usually more useful than long theory sessions. Training should use the organization’s own reports and examples so managers can immediately apply the concepts. Refresher sessions should follow major policy changes, audit findings, or recurring control weaknesses.

Study instruction:
- "Focus training on the financial decisions program managers make regularly rather than on technical accounting procedures they do not own."
Key point:
- Practical financial literacy should be linked to actual management decisions.
- Training should reinforce controls, forecasting, reporting, and accountability.

14. Q: How would you explain financial risks to a project leader without overwhelming them with accounting terminology?
A: Show answer
I would frame the risk in terms of what could happen to the project and what decision is needed. Instead of saying that a budget category has a classification issue, Finance could say that an expenditure may be charged to the wrong funding line and could therefore be rejected in donor reporting. Instead of explaining an accounting technicality at length, the report should identify the risk, its likely consequence, the evidence needed, the control required, and who should act. A traffic-light or exception-based report can help distinguish urgent items from routine items, but the underlying reasoning should remain documented. This keeps communication simple while preserving financial substance.

Study instruction:
- "Translate technical financial risks into operational consequences, required actions, evidence, and escalation points."
Key point:
- Risk communication should explain consequence and action, not just technical terminology.
- Managers need enough information to make a controlled decision.

- UNICEF — Risk Management, Budget Monitoring and Compliance Support
- USAID Office of Inspector General — Monitoring and Corrective Action Recommendations
15. Q: How would you establish financial accountability in a project without creating excessive bureaucracy?
A: Show answer
I would focus controls on the decisions that create the greatest financial exposure. Program managers should know their approval limits, required supporting evidence, budget availability requirements, procurement rules, donor restrictions, and escalation thresholds. The process should use standard templates and automated checks where appropriate so that staff do not repeatedly recreate the same control. At the same time, Finance should avoid requiring multiple approvals for low-risk routine items when one appropriate control is sufficient. The goal is not the maximum number of signatures; it is a clear chain of responsibility, evidence, segregation of duties, and proportionate review.

Study instruction:
- "Design controls around risk and decision points rather than adding administrative steps without a clear control purpose."
Key point:
- Strong control does not necessarily mean more paperwork.
- Proportionate controls can improve accountability while preserving operational efficiency.
Source links:
- USAID — Financial Management Manual
- USAID Office of Inspector General — Internal Control and Corrective Action
16. Q: What controls should program managers understand when approving expenditure?
A: Show answer
Managers should understand the controls that affect whether the expenditure is appropriate before they approve it. These commonly include confirmation that the activity is within the approved work plan, sufficient budget exists, the correct project or donor code is used, the required procurement process has been followed, the supplier or beneficiary relationship is appropriate, supporting documents exist, the expenditure is allowable under relevant funding terms, and conflicts of interest or other control concerns have been addressed. Managers do not need to perform every Finance verification, but they must complete the checks assigned to their role. Clear approval checklists can make this expectation practical.

Study instruction:
- "Learn the pre-approval questions that connect program decisions to budget, procurement, eligibility, documentation, and delegation controls."
Key point:
- Approval should confirm more than the presence of an invoice.
- Program managers need clearly defined control responsibilities.

17. Q: How would you support a manager whose project is consistently underspending?
A: Show answer
I would avoid treating underspending as automatically positive. First, Finance should compare actual expenditure with the implementation plan, milestones, procurement pipeline, staffing plan, partner activities, and project end date. The question is whether the lower spending reflects savings, delayed activities, procurement problems, under-delivery, or unrealistic budgeting. If activities are simply delayed, the forecast and implementation schedule should be updated. If expected savings are structural, the manager may need to discuss whether resources can be reallocated within the applicable rules. If underspending indicates that agreed outputs will not be delivered, senior management or the donor may need to be informed according to the applicable agreement.

Study instruction:
- "Interpret underspending together with implementation performance before deciding whether it represents efficiency, timing, or under-delivery."
Key point:
- Low expenditure is not automatically good performance.
- Financial utilization should be considered alongside program results and timing.
Source links:
- National Council of Nonprofits — Budget Review and Planning
- UNICEF — Results-Based Management Handbook
18. Q: How would you support a manager whose project is overspending?
A: Show answer
I would first establish whether the overspend is real, correctly coded, authorized, and expected to continue. Finance should review the affected budget line, transactions, commitments, timing, procurement records, staffing changes, and operational explanation. The manager should then identify whether the overspend is caused by higher quantities, higher prices, unexpected implementation requirements, incorrect allocation, or poor spending control. Immediate actions may include stopping discretionary expenditure, revising the forecast, moving resources within approved rules, seeking formal budget revision, or escalating the issue. The response should address both the current variance and the underlying cause so that the problem does not simply reappear in the following month.

Study instruction:
- "Use transaction-level evidence and operational explanations to separate genuine overspending from coding or timing issues."
Key point:
- Overspending requires both immediate containment and root-cause analysis.
- Corrective action must respect the organization’s and donor’s approval rules.

19. Q: What would you do if a program manager says that Finance reports are too complicated to use?
A: Show answer
I would treat that as a reporting-design problem to investigate rather than simply telling the manager to learn Finance. I would ask which decisions the manager needs to make, which report sections they use, which terms are unclear, and which information is missing. The report could then be redesigned into a management dashboard showing a small number of relevant measures, while keeping detailed schedules available as supporting tabs. Definitions and brief commentary can explain material variances. The objective is not to remove financial detail altogether but to create layers: a concise management view for routine decisions and a detailed evidence layer for Finance, audit, and follow-up.

Study instruction:
- "Evaluate financial reports from the user’s decision needs and create summary and detailed layers rather than one overloaded report."
Key point:
- A difficult report may indicate poor design rather than poor financial literacy.
- Management reporting should be concise at the top and traceable underneath.

20. Q: How would you distinguish between a genuine program-performance problem and a financial-reporting problem?
A: Show answer
I would compare the financial data with operational evidence. A program can have low expenditure because implementation is delayed, but the accounting may still be accurate. Conversely, expenditure may be recorded against a project and create an apparently healthy utilization rate even though it has been posted to the wrong activity. Finance should therefore reconcile financial results to activity milestones, procurement status, staffing, partner reports, and other program evidence. If the financial records are accurate but delivery is behind schedule, the issue is primarily operational. If delivery is occurring but the accounting does not represent it correctly, the issue is financial reporting or data quality. Sometimes both problems exist and require coordinated action.

Study instruction:
- "Compare financial evidence with program implementation evidence before assigning responsibility for a performance issue."
Key point:
- Financial utilization alone cannot explain program performance.
- Financial and operational evidence should be analyzed together.

- UNDP — Monitor Performance Against Programme and Operational Targets
- UNICEF — Results-Based Management Handbook
21. Q: How would you connect financial performance with program results?
A: Show answer
I would organize financial review around outputs, milestones, and resources rather than around ledger accounts alone. For example, if a project planned to train 1,000 people using $100,000, a useful management review can examine how much has been spent, how many participants have been reached, what remains to be delivered, and the expected final cost. This helps management consider cost efficiency, implementation progress, and resource requirements together. Finance should not claim that lower cost automatically means better performance, because quality and outcomes also matter. Instead, financial analysis should provide evidence that helps managers understand how resources are being converted into planned activities and results.

Study instruction:
- "Link expenditure to outputs, milestones, and resources while avoiding the assumption that cheaper automatically means better."
Key point:
- Financial performance should be interpreted alongside program results.
- Cost information is most useful when connected to delivery and outcomes.

22. Q: How would you explain cost efficiency and value for money to a program manager?
A: Show answer
Cost efficiency asks how much resource is being used to produce a given level of activity or output, while value-for-money discussions also require attention to quality, effectiveness, and appropriateness. A manager should therefore avoid judging a procurement decision solely because it has the lowest unit price. Finance can compare planned and actual unit costs, analyze major cost drivers, and help identify whether resources are being used as intended. The analysis should then be discussed with program staff because a higher cost may be justified by quality, access, location, urgency, or other program requirements. The purpose is disciplined resource use rather than indiscriminate cost cutting.

Study instruction:
- "Separate simple cost reduction from broader value-for-money analysis that considers cost, quality, effectiveness, and context."
Key point:
- The lowest price is not automatically the best financial decision.
- Cost analysis should support informed program choices.
Source links:
- UNICEF — Cost Efficiency and Value for Money Analysis
- UNDP — Financial Performance and Cost-Saving Strategies
23. Q: What common financial-management mistakes do non-finance managers make?
A: Show answer
Common problems include committing expenditure before checking budget availability, assuming approved budgets can be spent regardless of timing, confusing cash availability with budget availability, overlooking commitments, charging costs to the wrong project or donor, submitting incomplete supporting evidence, delaying explanations for variances, and failing to update forecasts when operational plans change. Another mistake is treating Finance as responsible for detecting every issue after the fact. These problems can often be reduced through clear guidance, simple approval checklists, timely reports, and practical training. Finance should also look for patterns in recurring mistakes rather than repeatedly correcting the same transaction without addressing the process that caused it.

Study instruction:
- "Identify recurring manager-level errors and connect each one to a preventive control, training intervention, or reporting improvement."
Key point:
- Repeated errors usually indicate a process or understanding problem.
- Preventive controls are more effective than repeated after-the-fact corrections alone.

24. Q: How would you respond when a program manager wants to spend outside the approved budget?
A: Show answer
I would first determine why the requested spending differs from the approved plan. The manager may have identified a legitimate operational need, a changed implementation assumption, or an error in the original budget. Finance should identify whether the proposed cost can be absorbed within an existing budget category, whether reallocation is permitted, and whether donor or internal approval is required. The manager should not be told merely that the budget line is unavailable without discussing the available options. At the same time, Finance should not normalize retrospective approvals. The correct approach is to establish the rule before commitment or expenditure and document the decision and authorization.

Study instruction:
- "Check budget authority, reallocation rules, donor conditions, and approval requirements before an out-of-budget commitment is made."
Key point:
- An operational need does not automatically create budget authority.
- Exceptions should be addressed through the correct approval process before spending where possible.

25. Q: What would you check if a project’s financial report shows an unexpected large variance?
A: Show answer
I would start with the underlying transaction population and determine whether the variance is genuine. Checks should include opening balances, budget version, expenditure postings, account and project coding, timing of invoices, accruals, reversals, reallocations, commitments, and any recent corrections. I would then compare the result with operational events such as procurement, recruitment, partner payments, or major activities. If the variance is valid, I would identify the cause and forecast impact. If it results from a coding or reporting issue, the correction should be made with appropriate evidence and authorization. The investigation should be documented so that the same question does not have to be reconstructed later.

Study instruction:
- "Investigate large variances through both transaction-level evidence and operational explanations."
Key point:
- Unexpected variances require validation before interpretation.
- Documentation of the investigation is part of financial control.

26. Q: How would you handle a program manager who repeatedly submits poor financial forecasts?
A: Show answer
I would first determine whether the problem is lack of information, lack of forecasting skill, unrealistic assumptions, poor communication about the implementation plan, or insufficient review time. Finance can introduce a standard forecasting template that requires the manager to state remaining activities, expected timing, known commitments, key assumptions, and risks. Forecasts should then be compared with previous forecasts and actual results so the team can understand where the assumptions repeatedly fail. The objective should be improvement rather than simply assigning blame. If forecast quality remains materially weak, management may need to introduce additional review or require Finance to validate certain assumptions before the forecast is finalized.

Study instruction:
- "Improve forecasting by documenting assumptions and comparing previous forecasts with actual outcomes."
Key point:
- Forecast accuracy improves when assumptions are visible and tested.
- Repeated forecast errors should trigger process improvement, not only correction of individual numbers.

27. Q: How would you communicate a financial problem to senior management when the program team has not yet resolved it?
A: Show answer
The escalation should be factual, concise, and action-oriented. I would state what the issue is, the amount or exposure involved, when it was identified, what evidence has been reviewed, the likely operational or donor consequence, what the program manager has said, and what action is now required. Senior management should not receive a long transaction listing without an explanation of significance. Where uncertainty remains, that should be stated clearly. The escalation should also identify the decision needed from senior management, such as approval of a corrective action, resource allocation, donor discussion, or additional review. This makes escalation a management tool rather than a complaint about the program team.

Study instruction:
- "Structure escalations around issue, evidence, consequence, response to date, uncertainty, and decision required."
Key point:
- Senior management needs decision-ready financial information.
- Escalation should clarify risk without overstating certainty.

28. Q: How would you handle disagreement between a program manager and Finance over a financial issue?
A: Show answer
I would separate the disagreement into facts, interpretation, policy requirements, and operational judgment. First, both parties should agree on the underlying facts: what happened, what was spent, what was approved, and what evidence exists. Finance should then explain the accounting or policy basis for its position, while the program manager explains the operational rationale. If the issue depends on donor terms or organizational policy, the relevant rule should be reviewed rather than resolved through personal opinion. If genuine uncertainty remains, it should be escalated to the appropriate authority and documented. The relationship should remain professional because a disagreement can reveal an important control or policy gap that the organization should fix.

Study instruction:
- "Resolve disagreements by separating facts, rules, operational judgment, and decision authority."
Key point:
- Disagreement should be resolved through evidence and defined authority.
- Unclear policies should be clarified rather than informally bypassed.

29. Q: How would you help managers understand restricted donor funding?
A: Show answer
I would explain restrictions in operational terms: which activities, people, locations, cost categories, time periods, or outputs the funding can support, and what approvals are required to change those uses. A manager should understand that having money available in a bank account does not necessarily mean it can be spent on any project need. Finance should maintain a grant schedule showing approved budget, expenditure, commitments, remaining funding, key restrictions, reporting dates, and expiry dates where applicable. Before significant spending decisions, Finance and the manager should confirm the funding source and eligibility. This prevents situations where a cost is operationally reasonable but financially unsupported under the grant agreement.

Study instruction:
- "Interpret donor restrictions as decision rules that affect what the program may fund, when it may fund it, and what evidence is required."
Key point:
- Funding availability and funding eligibility are different questions.
- Managers need visibility over restrictions that affect operational choices.

30. Q: How would you help a project leader understand donor-reporting implications?
A: Show answer
The manager should know what financial information will ultimately be required in donor reporting and how current operational decisions affect that information. Finance can provide a reporting calendar, explain eligible cost requirements, identify important budget categories, and highlight changes that require donor approval or formal explanation. The project team should maintain documentation that supports the financial and programmatic claims being reported. Finance should also reconcile donor reporting figures to accounting records and investigate differences before submission. This makes donor reporting an ongoing management process rather than a last-minute exercise carried out only when the report deadline arrives.

31. Q: What evidence should program managers retain to support financial performance?
A: Show answer
The evidence should demonstrate that the expenditure or commitment was authorized, related to the approved activity, properly procured where required, received or incurred as represented, and correctly classified. Depending on the transaction, this can include purchase requests, approvals, quotations or procurement records, contracts, purchase orders, delivery evidence, invoices, attendance records, travel documentation, partner reports, activity completion evidence, and other supporting documents. Program managers should know which evidence they are expected to confirm or provide and should understand retention requirements. Finance should define the evidence standard clearly rather than asking managers to guess what is sufficient after the transaction has already occurred.

Study instruction:
- "Map each major expenditure type to the evidence needed to demonstrate authorization, occurrence, eligibility, and correct classification."
Key point:
- Financial accountability depends on reliable evidence as well as correct numbers.
- Evidence requirements should be defined before transactions are processed.

36. Q: How would you determine whether a financial problem is caused by planning or execution?

38. Q: How would you help managers understand the difference between a financial transaction problem and a financial performance problem?
