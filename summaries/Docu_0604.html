2. Q: What is an incentive in the economic sense used by the chapter?
A: Show answer
An incentive is a benefit, cost, reward, penalty or other consequence that changes the attractiveness of an action. It can encourage someone to do more of a desired activity or less of an undesired one. Incentives include explicit payments and fines, but also reputation, approval, shame, personal values and the prospect of being caught. They matter because decisions are made within a setting of competing needs and constraints. The relevant question is not only what a person says they value, but also how the available consequences affect the choice they make.

4. Q: What are the three broad categories of incentives identified in the chapter?
A: Show answer
The chapter distinguishes economic, social and moral incentives. Economic incentives involve material gains or losses, such as wages, bonuses, prices, taxes and fines. Social incentives arise from approval, status, belonging, reputation, embarrassment or the possibility of public disapproval. Moral incentives operate through a person’s sense of right and wrong, duty, guilt, fairness or concern for others. A single arrangement can activate all three categories at once, and their effects may reinforce or undermine one another.

5. Q: How can economic, social and moral incentives interact within one policy?
A: Show answer
A policy can offer a financial reward, signal what the group considers acceptable and communicate whether the behaviour is ethically important. For example, a smoking tax raises the monetary cost of cigarettes, smoke-free rules change the social setting, and public health messages appeal to personal responsibility. These mechanisms may reinforce each other, but they can also conflict. A payment may weaken a moral norm if people begin to see a previously voluntary duty as a commercial transaction. Designers should therefore examine the combined message rather than evaluating each incentive in isolation.

6. Q: How was the day-care late-pickup experiment structured?
A: Show answer
The economists studied ten day-care centres in Haifa over twenty weeks. During the first four weeks they recorded late pickups without imposing a fine; the average was about eight late pickups per centre per week. From the fifth week, parents arriving more than ten minutes late were charged three dollars per child per incident, added to a monthly bill of roughly 380 dollars. The researchers then observed behaviour after the fee was introduced and after it was later withdrawn. The design made it possible to compare behaviour across different stages of the policy, although it was a field study in a limited setting rather than a universal test of every possible fine.

9. Q: What is the difference between a fine that deters behaviour and a fee that effectively prices the behaviour?
A: Show answer
A deterrent penalty is intended to make the unwanted action sufficiently costly that people avoid it. A fee can instead communicate that the action is permitted if the person is willing to pay. The distinction depends on context, amount, enforcement and the meaning of the underlying rule. Calling a charge a fine does not guarantee that people will perceive it as a moral or legal prohibition.

13. Q: Why is every incentive described as a trade-off?

16. Q: What is the motivation crowding-out effect?
A: Show answer
Motivation crowding-out occurs when an external reward, penalty or controlling intervention weakens a person’s existing intrinsic or prosocial motivation. Someone who volunteers because the activity feels meaningful may respond differently when it becomes a paid task with strict monitoring. The effect is not automatic: external incentives can also strengthen motivation when they communicate appreciation, support competence or remove practical barriers. The relevant question is how the intervention changes autonomy, trust, perceived purpose and the relationship between the person and the activity.

18. Q: How can a designer distinguish an incentive effect from a change in the surrounding environment?
