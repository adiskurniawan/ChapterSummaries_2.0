A: Show answer
The chapter calls it the age of AI because artificial intelligence has moved from a specialist research topic into ordinary services and consequential public and commercial systems. 2. Q: What is the difference between narrow AI, general-purpose AI, and artificial general intelligence?
A: Show answer
Narrow AI is designed or trained to perform a limited class of tasks, such as image classification, search ranking, translation, or playing a particular game. A system may perform one task exceptionally well without being able to transfer its competence reliably to unrelated tasks. General-purpose AI is used in the chapter to describe a more flexible system able to converse, combine ideas across subject areas, and adapt to unfamiliar problems. Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is the more specific idea of machine intelligence with broad flexibility and competence comparable in important respects to human intelligence, including learning across domains and applying common sense. These labels are debated and do not yet have one universally accepted operational threshold; capability should therefore be assessed by demonstrated performance, transfer, reliability, and autonomy rather than by a label alone.

Key point:

5. Q: What is superintelligent AI, and how does it differ from AGI?
A: Show answer

AGI, by contrast, is generally framed as broad or human-comparable intelligence. Superintelligence therefore represents a further and more demanding possibility: superior performance across a wide range of cognitive activities, potentially including scientific discovery, strategic planning, social prediction, and the design of more capable systems. The distinction matters because risks associated with systems that exceed human oversight may be qualitatively different from those associated with task-specific automation.

Forecasts should be revisited as evidence changes, and decision-makers should prepare for a range of plausible outcomes rather than betting on one date. The chapter's own caution about radical uncertainty reinforces this approach.

18. Q: What is the “Jetsons Fallacy”?
A: Show answer
The Jetsons Fallacy is the assumption that AI will transform the surrounding world while human beings themselves remain unchanged. The chapter uses familiar science-fiction images in which people live alongside sophisticated machines but continue to have ordinary, unmodified human minds and bodies. The author argues that this picture overlooks the possibility that AI development will also stimulate technologies that alter human cognition, health, communication, or physical capability. The fallacy is therefore a failure to consider reciprocal transformation: technology changes people, and people adapt themselves through technology. It is a conceptual warning, not a prediction that every person will adopt brain implants.

22. Q: What is transhumanism?

Transhumanism is a philosophical and cultural movement that supports using science and technology to overcome or extend some present human limitations. In the chapter, it is associated with the possibility of radically improved intelligence, longer life, stronger health, new forms of relationship with AI, and elective changes to the body. The term covers diverse views, so it should not be treated as one uniform programme.

34. Q: What is Integrated Information Theory (IIT) in the context of this chapter?
A: Show answer
IIT, associated with Giulio Tononi and collaborators, proposes that consciousness is related to integrated information in a physical system. The theory begins from features of experience and derives requirements for mechanisms that could support it.
